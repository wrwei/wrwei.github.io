Beyond propositional logic

Module 2 treated every statement as an indivisible atom. That is enough for rules such as “if the door is open and the alarm is armed, the siren sounds”. Most requirements, though, talk about many objects at once: every request, some server, no two trains, the owner of a file. Here is an access-control policy and a conclusion that anyone would draw from it:

  1. Every administrator can delete every document.
  2. Alice is an administrator, and the quarterly report is a document.
  3. Therefore Alice can delete the quarterly report.

In propositional logic each sentence becomes an atom, say AAA⁠, BBB and CCC⁠, and the argument becomes the formula A∧B⟶CA \land B \imp CA∧B⟶C⁠. Its truth table:

TTTT
TTFF
TFTT
TFFT
FTTT
FTFT
FFTT
FFFT

Satisfiable but not valid: true in 7 rows.

The second row makes the formula false: AAA and BBB true, CCC false. As far as propositional logic can tell, the conclusion does not follow. Yet it does, for reasons that lie inside the sentences: the Alice of sentence 2 is the Alice of sentence 3, “every administrator” in sentence 1 includes her, and “every document” includes the report. To capture such reasons, a logic needs

  • names for objects, such as Alice and the report;
  • properties and relations of objects, such as “is an administrator” and “can delete”;
  • quantifiers, the words of quantity “every” and “some”, with variables that range over the objects.

Here predicate logic means first-order logic: its variables range over objects, rather than over predicates or relations themselves. It adds the vocabulary and quantifiers above to the connectives of Module 2. Gottlob Frege introduced quantifiers in his Begriffsschrift of 1879, and predicate logic has since become the standard language for stating mathematics precisely. Written in it, the whole argument is one formula:

(∀u d. admin u∧doc d⟶can_delete u d)∧admin alice∧doc report⟶can_delete alice report(\forall u\ d.\ \mathit{admin}\ u \land \mathit{doc}\ d \imp \mathit{can\_delete}\ u\ d) \land \mathit{admin}\ \mathit{alice} \land \mathit{doc}\ \mathit{report} \imp \mathit{can\_delete}\ \mathit{alice}\ \mathit{report} (∀u d. admin u∧doc d⟶can_delete u d)∧admin alice∧doc report⟶can_delete alice report

Here doc d\mathit{doc}\ ddoc d means “ddd is a document”. Read the formula as “if every administrator can delete every document, Alice is an administrator, and the report is a document, then Alice can delete the report”. By the end of this module you will know what it means for this formula to be true however its words are interpreted, and Isabelle will prove it in one line (Section 3.8).

Predicate logic is one language for specifications. The sorting condition of Module 1, sorted ys∧mset ys=mset xs\mathit{sorted}\ ys \land \mathit{mset}\ ys = \mathit{mset}\ xssorted ys∧mset ys=mset xs⁠, uses predicates, functions and equality in this way. Isabelle supplies their typed definitions in its richer logic, HOL.

Terms, predicates and formulas

A formula of predicate logic is built from two kinds of symbol. The logical symbols are the same in every application: the connectives of Module 2, the quantifiers, equality, and variables. The other symbols name the things of one particular application, such as users, documents and permissions. They form its vocabulary.

Definition 3.1Vocabulary

A vocabulary (also called a signature) lists the non-logical symbols of a language, each with its number of arguments, its arity:

  • constants name single objects: alice\mathit{alice}alice⁠, report\mathit{report}report⁠, 000⁠;
  • function symbols name operations that give an object: owner\mathit{owner}owner (one argument: the owner of a document), +++ (two arguments);
  • predicate symbols name properties and relations, which hold or do not hold: admin\mathit{admin}admin (one argument), can_delete\mathit{can\_delete}can_delete (two), <<< (two).

Most logic books write P(x)P(x)P(x)⁠, R(x,y)R(x, y)R(x,y) and f(x)f(x)f(x)⁠. This series writes them as Isabelle does: the symbol, then its arguments, separated by spaces: P xP\ xP x⁠, R x yR\ x\ yR x y⁠, f xf\ xf x⁠. Parentheses only group: can_delete (owner d) d\mathit{can\_delete}\ (\mathit{owner}\ d)\ dcan_delete (owner d) d applies can_delete\mathit{can\_delete}can_delete to two arguments, owner d\mathit{owner}\ downer d and ddd⁠. Symbols such as +++ and <<< stay between their arguments, as usual.

Terms

Definition 3.2Term

A term is either

  1. a variable (xxx⁠, yyy⁠, zzz⁠, …) or a constant, or
  2. f t1 … tnf\ t_1\ \dots\ t_nf t1​ … tn​⁠, where fff is a function symbol with nnn arguments and t1,…,tnt_1, \dots, t_nt1​,…,tn​ are terms.

A term names an object. It is never true or false.

alice\mathit{alice}alice⁠, ddd⁠, owner d\mathit{owner}\ downer d⁠, x+1x + 1x+1 and 2∗(x+y)2 * (x + y)2∗(x+y) are terms. owner d\mathit{owner}\ downer d names the owner of whichever document ddd stands for, and x+1x + 1x+1 names a number. Terms are like the expressions of a programming language in that they denote values. An interpretation need not supply an executable algorithm for computing them.

Atomic formulas

Definition 3.3Atomic formula

An atomic formula is either P t1 … tnP\ t_1\ \dots\ t_nP t1​ … tn​⁠, where PPP is a predicate symbol with nnn arguments and t1,…,tnt_1, \dots, t_nt1​,…,tn​ are terms, or an equation t1=t2t_1 = t_2t1​=t2​ between two terms.

admin alice\mathit{admin}\ \mathit{alice}admin alice⁠, can_delete x report\mathit{can\_delete}\ x\ \mathit{report}can_delete x report⁠, x<y+1x < y + 1x<y+1 and owner report=alice\mathit{owner}\ \mathit{report} = \mathit{alice}owner report=alice are atomic formulas. Each becomes true or false once you know which objects its terms stand for. A predicate symbol with no arguments is a propositional atom of Module 2, so every formula of propositional logic is also a formula of predicate logic.

Watch out

Terms and formulas live on different levels, and one cannot stand where the other belongs. admin (can_delete alice report)\mathit{admin}\ (\mathit{can\_delete}\ \mathit{alice}\ \mathit{report})admin (can_delete alice report) is not a formula: admin\mathit{admin}admin needs an object, and can_delete alice report\mathit{can\_delete}\ \mathit{alice}\ \mathit{report}can_delete alice report is a statement. Nor is owner report∧admin alice\mathit{owner}\ \mathit{report} \land \mathit{admin}\ \mathit{alice}owner report∧admin alice⁠: ∧\land∧ joins statements, and owner report\mathit{owner}\ \mathit{report}owner report is an object. Isabelle rejects such expressions, as Section 3.8 shows.

Quantifiers

The new ingredient is quantification over objects. Here φ\varphiφ (the Greek letter phi) and ψ\psiψ (psi) stand for any formulas.

  • ∀x. φ\forall x.\ \varphi∀x. φ reads “for all xxx⁠, φ\varphiφ”. It says that φ\varphiφ holds whichever object xxx stands for. ∀\forall∀ is the universal quantifier.
  • ∃x. φ\exists x.\ \varphi∃x. φ reads “there exists an xxx such that φ\varphiφ”, or “for some xxx⁠, φ\varphiφ”. It says that φ\varphiφ holds for at least one object. ∃\exists∃ is the existential quantifier.

One quantifier may introduce several variables: ∀x y. φ\forall x\ y.\ \varphi∀x y. φ is short for ∀x. ∀y. φ\forall x.\ \forall y.\ \varphi∀x. ∀y. φ⁠.

Definition 3.4Formula

The formulas over a vocabulary are built by these rules:

  1. Every atomic formula is a formula, and so are True\mathit{True}True and False\mathit{False}False⁠.
  2. If φ\varphiφ and ψ\psiψ are formulas, so are ¬φ\lnot \varphi¬φ⁠, φ∧ψ\varphi \land \psiφ∧ψ⁠, φ∨ψ\varphi \lor \psiφ∨ψ⁠, φ⟶ψ\varphi \imp \psiφ⟶ψ and φ⟷ψ\varphi \iff \psiφ⟷ψ⁠, with parentheses where the priorities of Module 2 need them.
  3. If φ\varphiφ is a formula and xxx is a variable, then ∀x. φ\forall x.\ \varphi∀x. φ and ∃x. φ\exists x.\ \varphi∃x. φ are formulas.

Reading formulas fluently takes practice. Read them aloud, symbol by symbol, then say what they mean in plain English.

Scope, free and bound variables

In ∀x. φ\forall x.\ \varphi∀x. φ the formula φ\varphiφ is the scope of the quantifier: the region in which xxx refers to it. When a formula has no parentheses, how far does a scope extend? This series follows Isabelle: the scope of a quantifier extends as far to the right as possible. So ∀x. P x⟶Q x\forall x.\ P\ x \imp Q\ x∀x. P x⟶Q x means ∀x. (P x⟶Q x)\forall x.\ (P\ x \imp Q\ x)∀x. (P x⟶Q x)⁠. To end a scope earlier, use parentheses: (∀x. P x)⟶Q x(\forall x.\ P\ x) \imp Q\ x(∀x. P x)⟶Q x⁠.

Watch out

Many logic textbooks use the opposite convention: there a quantifier binds as tightly as ¬\lnot¬⁠, and ∀x P(x)⟶Q(x)\forall x\, P(x) \imp Q(x)∀xP(x)⟶Q(x) means (∀x P(x))⟶Q(x)(\forall x\, P(x)) \imp Q(x)(∀xP(x))⟶Q(x)⁠. When you read other books, check their convention. When you write for Isabelle, remember that a quantifier’s scope runs to the end of the formula, or to the closing parenthesis of the group the quantifier stands in.

Definition 3.5Free and bound variables

An occurrence of a variable xxx in a formula is bound if it lies in the scope of a quantifier ∀x\forall x∀x or ∃x\exists x∃x⁠; it then belongs to the nearest such quantifier around it. Otherwise the occurrence is free. A formula in which no variable occurs free is a sentence, or closed formula.

Figure 3.1 marks the scopes in a formula in which the same variable name occurs both bound and free.

A sentence is true or false once its symbols have a meaning. A formula with free variables also depends on what they stand for: x<3x < 3x<3 is neither true nor false until xxx has a value, while ∃x. x<3\exists x.\ x < 3∃x. x<3 is a sentence, true of the natural numbers. A free variable is like a parameter of a function, a bound variable like a local variable: renaming a bound variable throughout its scope, with a fresh name, changes nothing, so ∀x. P x\forall x.\ P\ x∀x. P x and ∀y. P y\forall y.\ P\ y∀y. P y say the same thing.

Example 3.2Finding scopes and free variables
  1. ∀x. P x⟶Q x\forall x.\ P\ x \imp Q\ x∀x. P x⟶Q x⁠: the scope of ∀x\forall x∀x is P x⟶Q xP\ x \imp Q\ xP x⟶Q x⁠, and both occurrences of xxx are bound. A sentence.
  2. (∀x. P x)⟶Q x(\forall x.\ P\ x) \imp Q\ x(∀x. P x)⟶Q x⁠: the scope is only P xP\ xP x⁠. The xxx in Q xQ\ xQ x is free.
  3. ∀x. ∃y. R x y∧¬ R y z\forall x.\ \exists y.\ R\ x\ y \land \lnot\, R\ y\ z∀x. ∃y. R x y∧¬R y z⁠: xxx and yyy are bound, zzz is free. Not a sentence.

The parentheses are gone: the scope of ∀x runs to the end anyway, so they changed nothing. Abandon the lemma with oops and move the parenthesis:

3

Structures and truth

Is ∀x. ∃y. R x y\forall x.\ \exists y.\ R\ x\ y∀x. ∃y. R x y true? The question has no answer yet. If xxx and yyy range over the natural numbers and RRR is <<<⁠, it is true: every number has a larger one. If RRR is >>>⁠, it is false: no natural number is smaller than 0. A formula gets a truth value only once you say what its variables range over and what its symbols mean. That information is a structure.

Definition 3.6Structure

A structure M\mathcal MM for a vocabulary consists of

  1. a non-empty set DDD⁠, the domain: the objects that variables range over;
  2. for each constant ccc⁠, an element cMc^{\mathcal M}cM of DDD⁠;
  3. for each function symbol fff with nnn arguments, a function fMf^{\mathcal M}fM that maps every nnn-tuple of elements of DDD to an element of DDD⁠;
  4. for each predicate symbol PPP with nnn arguments, a set PMP^{\mathcal M}PM of nnn-tuples of elements of DDD⁠: the tuples for which PPP holds.

An nnn-tuple is a list (d1,…,dn)(d_1, \dots, d_n)(d1​,…,dn​) of nnn elements, in order; a 2-tuple is a pair. Read cMc^{\mathcal M}cM as “ccc in M\mathcal MM”: what M\mathcal MM makes the symbol ccc mean; likewise fMf^{\mathcal M}fM and PMP^{\mathcal M}PM⁠. Items 2 to 4 are the interpretation of the vocabulary. Structures are also called models or interpretations.

The domain may be finite or infinite, but by convention it is never empty; Isabelle follows the same convention. A function symbol is interpreted by a function that gives a value for every argument, even for arguments where the value makes no sense, such as the owner of something that is not a document. Section 3.8 shows how types avoid such nonsense.

To evaluate a formula with free variables, you also need values for them.

Definition 3.7Truth in a structure

An assignment sss gives every variable a value in DDD⁠. The value of a term is computed from the values of its variables and constants, using the functions of M\mathcal MM⁠. A formula is true in M\mathcal MM under sss according to these rules:

  • P t1 … tnP\ t_1\ \dots\ t_nP t1​ … tn​ is true when the tuple of the values of t1,…,tnt_1, \dots, t_nt1​,…,tn​ is in PMP^{\mathcal M}PM⁠; t1=t2t_1 = t_2t1​=t2​ is true when the two terms have the same value.
  • True\mathit{True}True is true and False\mathit{False}False is false; ¬\lnot¬⁠, ∧\land∧⁠, ∨\lor∨⁠, ⟶\imp⟶ and ⟷\iff⟷ follow their truth tables from Module 2.
  • ∀x. φ\forall x.\ \varphi∀x. φ is true when φ\varphiφ is true for every value of xxx in DDD⁠, the other variables keeping their values.
  • ∃x. φ\exists x.\ \varphi∃x. φ is true when φ\varphiφ is true for at least one value of xxx in DDD⁠.

For a sentence the assignment makes no difference. You then write M⊨φ\mathcal M \models \varphiM⊨φ⁠, read “φ\varphiφ is true in M\mathcal MM” or “M\mathcal MM is a model of φ\varphiφ”.

Think of it like this

Over a finite domain, a quantifier is a loop. ∀x\forall x∀x is a loop that succeeds only if every round succeeds; ∃x\exists x∃x succeeds as soon as one round does. In Python, ∀x. ∃y. R x y\forall x.\ \exists y.\ R\ x\ y∀x. ∃y. R x y over a list D is all(any(R(x, y) for y in D) for x in D). Over an infinite domain, enumeration is not a general decision procedure: a search may find a witness or a counterexample, but it cannot finish inspecting every element. General reasoning can settle claims that enumeration cannot.

Now an example by hand. Figure 3.2 draws a structure for a vocabulary with two one-place predicates, PPP and QQQ⁠, and a two-place predicate RRR⁠.

Written out, M1\mathcal M_1M1​ has the domain D={a,b,c}D = \{a, b, c\}D={a,b,c}⁠, with PM1={a,b}P^{\mathcal M_1} = \{a, b\}PM1​={a,b}⁠, QM1={b}Q^{\mathcal M_1} = \{b\}QM1​={b} and RM1={(a,b),(b,c),(c,c)}R^{\mathcal M_1} = \{(a, b), (b, c), (c, c)\}RM1​={(a,b),(b,c),(c,c)}⁠. Example 3.4The order of quantifiers

In M1\mathcal M_1M1​⁠, the sentence ∀x. ∃y. R x y\forall x.\ \exists y.\ R\ x\ y∀x. ∃y. R x y says that every element has an outgoing arrow. It does: a→ba \to ba→b⁠, b→cb \to cb→c and c→cc \to cc→c⁠. Each xxx has its own witness yyy⁠.

∃y. ∀x. R x y\exists y.\ \forall x.\ R\ x\ y∃y. ∀x. R x y says that some element receives an arrow from every element, itself included. Try each candidate yyy⁠: aaa receives no arrow; bbb receives an arrow from aaa only; ccc receives arrows from bbb and ccc⁠, but not from aaa⁠. No candidate works, so the sentence is false.

The two sentences differ only in the order of ∀x\forall x∀x and ∃y\exists y∃y⁠. In the first, yyy may depend on xxx⁠; in the second, one yyy must serve every xxx⁠.

A ∀-sentence can also be true for a less obvious reason. In a structure where no element has the property PPP⁠, the sentence ∀x. P x⟶Q x\forall x.\ P\ x \imp Q\ x∀x. P x⟶Q x is true whatever QQQ is: for every xxx the condition P xP\ xP x is false, so the implication is true. Such a sentence is vacuously true. “Every admin is on call” holds in a company without admins, and “every request was answered” holds for a server that received no requests. Specifications must reckon with this.

Check it in Isabelle

x::bool in a quantifier says that x ranges over bool. Every x has a y different from it, but no y is different from every x, because nothing is different from itself. A type with infinitely many elements, such as nat, cannot be run through: value "∀x::nat. x < x + 1" stops with an error saying that nat is not of sort enum, the class of types whose elements Isabelle can list. For such types you need a proof.

Validity, satisfiability and countermodels

The central notions of Module 2 carry over, with “every row of the truth table” replaced by “every structure”.

Definition 3.8Validity and satisfiability

A formula φ\varphiφ is

  • valid, written ⊨φ\models \varphi⊨φ⁠, if it is true in every structure under every assignment;
  • satisfiable if it is true in at least one structure under at least one assignment;
  • unsatisfiable if it is true in none.

A set of formulas Γ\GammaΓ (gamma) entails φ\varphiφ⁠, written Γ⊨φ\Gamma \models \varphiΓ⊨φ⁠, if φ\varphiφ is true in every structure and under every assignment that make all the formulas in Γ\GammaΓ true. φ\varphiφ is then a logical consequence of Γ\GammaΓ⁠.

As in Module 2, φ\varphiφ is valid exactly when ¬φ\lnot \varphi¬φ is unsatisfiable. The difference lies in the number of cases: a truth table has finitely many rows, but there are infinitely many structures, of every size, and you cannot inspect them all. To show that a formula is not valid, though, one structure is enough.

Definition 3.9Countermodel

A countermodel of φ\varphiφ is a structure, together with an assignment for the free variables of φ\varphiφ⁠, in which φ\varphiφ is false. A formula is valid exactly when it has no countermodel.

Example 3.5A countermodel

Is (∀x. P x∨Q x)⟶(∀x. P x)∨(∀x. Q x)(\forall x.\ P\ x \lor Q\ x) \imp (\forall x.\ P\ x) \lor (\forall x.\ Q\ x)(∀x. P x∨Q x)⟶(∀x. P x)∨(∀x. Q x) valid? It says: “if everything is PPP or QQQ⁠, then everything is PPP or everything is QQQ”. That sounds plausible until you picture one element that is only PPP and another that is only QQQ⁠: D={a,b}D = \{a, b\}D={a,b}⁠, P={a}P = \{a\}P={a}⁠, Q={b}Q = \{b\}Q={b}⁠. The premise is true, since aaa is PPP and bbb is QQQ⁠. But ∀x. P x\forall x.\ P\ x∀x. P x fails at bbb and ∀x. Q x\forall x.\ Q\ x∀x. Q x fails at aaa⁠, so the conclusion is false. This structure is a countermodel, and the formula is not valid. It is satisfiable, though: it is true in every structure where everything is PPP⁠.

To show that a formula is valid, you argue about an arbitrary structure: the argument may use nothing about the domain and the interpretation except what the formula itself says.

Example 3.6Proving validity

Claim: (∀x. P x⟶Q x)∧P c⟶Q c(\forall x.\ P\ x \imp Q\ x) \land P\ c \imp Q\ c(∀x. P x⟶Q x)∧P c⟶Q c is valid. Take any structure M\mathcal MM⁠. If the left-hand side of the implication is false in M\mathcal MM⁠, the implication is true. Otherwise both conjuncts are true. The first says that P x⟶Q xP\ x \imp Q\ xP x⟶Q x holds for every value of xxx⁠, in particular for the element cMc^{\mathcal M}cM⁠; the second says that PPP holds for cMc^{\mathcal M}cM⁠. Together they give Q cQ\ cQ c⁠. Either way the formula is true in M\mathcal MM⁠, and since M\mathcal MM was arbitrary, the formula is valid. This is the argument of Section 3.1 in miniature.

Valid formulas include every instance of a propositional tautology, such as P x∨¬ P xP\ x \lor \lnot\, P\ xP x∨¬P x⁠, and many that propositional logic cannot explain, such as (∀x. P x)⟶P c(\forall x.\ P\ x) \imp P\ c(∀x. P x)⟶P c⁠.

Reasoning with quantifiers

Two formulas are equivalent, written φ≡ψ\varphi \equiv \psiφ≡ψ⁠, if they are true in the same structures under the same assignments, that is, if φ⟷ψ\varphi \iff \psiφ⟷ψ is valid. Equivalences let you rewrite formulas: to simplify a specification, to negate a requirement, to follow a proof. This section collects the laws you will use most, and the ones that look right but fail.

Quantifier laws

Over a two-element domain {a,b}\{a, b\}{a,b}⁠, ∀x. P x\forall x.\ P\ x∀x. P x means P a∧P bP\ a \land P\ bP a∧P b⁠, and ∃x. P x\exists x.\ P\ x∃x. P x means P a∨P bP\ a \lor P\ bP a∨P b⁠. So ∀ behaves like a long conjunction and ∃ like a long disjunction, and the laws of Module 2 for ∧ and ∨ have quantifier versions.

Fact 3.1Negating quantifiers
¬(∀x. φ)≡∃x. ¬φ¬(∃x. φ)≡∀x. ¬φ\lnot (\forall x.\ \varphi) \equiv \exists x.\ \lnot \varphi \qquad\qquad \lnot (\exists x.\ \varphi) \equiv \forall x.\ \lnot \varphi ¬(∀x. φ)≡∃x. ¬φ¬(∃x. φ)≡∀x. ¬φ

“Not everything is φ\varphiφ” means “something is not φ\varphiφ”, and “nothing is φ\varphiφ” means “everything is not φ\varphiφ”. These are De Morgan’s laws for quantifiers. Pushing a negation inwards turns each quantifier it passes into the other one.

Example 3.7Negating a requirement

A requirement and its violation are negations of each other. Take “every request is answered”:

∀r. request r⟶answered r\forall r.\ \mathit{request}\ r \imp \mathit{answered}\ r∀r. request r⟶answered r

Its negation describes what a test, or a model checker, must find to show a violation:

¬(∀r. request r⟶answered r)  ≡  ∃r. ¬(request r⟶answered r)  ≡  ∃r. request r∧¬ answered r\lnot (\forall r.\ \mathit{request}\ r \imp \mathit{answered}\ r) \;\equiv\; \exists r.\ \lnot (\mathit{request}\ r \imp \mathit{answered}\ r) \;\equiv\; \exists r.\ \mathit{request}\ r \land \lnot\, \mathit{answered}\ r ¬(∀r. request r⟶answered r)≡∃r. ¬(request r⟶answered r)≡∃r. request r∧¬answered r

“Some request is not answered.” The last step uses ¬(A⟶B)≡A∧¬B\lnot (A \imp B) \equiv A \land \lnot B¬(A⟶B)≡A∧¬B from Module 2. With nested quantifiers the negation passes through each one in turn: ¬(∀x. ∃y. trusts x y)≡∃x. ∀y. ¬ trusts x y\lnot (\forall x.\ \exists y.\ \mathit{trusts}\ x\ y) \equiv \exists x.\ \forall y.\ \lnot\, \mathit{trusts}\ x\ y¬(∀x. ∃y. trusts x y)≡∃x. ∀y. ¬trusts x y⁠, “somebody trusts nobody”.

Fact 3.2Distribution
∀x. (φ∧ψ)≡(∀x. φ)∧(∀x. ψ)∃x. (φ∨ψ)≡(∃x. φ)∨(∃x. ψ)\forall x.\ (\varphi \land \psi) \equiv (\forall x.\ \varphi) \land (\forall x.\ \psi) \qquad\qquad \exists x.\ (\varphi \lor \psi) \equiv (\exists x.\ \varphi) \lor (\exists x.\ \psi) ∀x. (φ∧ψ)≡(∀x. φ)∧(∀x. ψ)∃x. (φ∨ψ)≡(∃x. φ)∨(∃x. ψ)

The other two combinations hold in one direction only:

(∀x. φ)∨(∀x. ψ)⊨∀x. (φ∨ψ)∃x. (φ∧ψ)⊨(∃x. φ)∧(∃x. ψ)(\forall x.\ \varphi) \lor (\forall x.\ \psi) \models \forall x.\ (\varphi \lor \psi) \qquad\qquad \exists x.\ (\varphi \land \psi) \models (\exists x.\ \varphi) \land (\exists x.\ \psi) (∀x. φ)∨(∀x. ψ)⊨∀x. (φ∨ψ)∃x. (φ∧ψ)⊨(∃x. φ)∧(∃x. ψ)

Example 3.5 showed that the first one-way law cannot be reversed. Nor can the second: “somebody is an admin and somebody is on call” does not mean that some admin is on call. The countermodel is again D={a,b}D = \{a, b\}D={a,b} with P={a}P = \{a\}P={a} and Q={b}Q = \{b\}Q={b}⁠.

Fact 3.3Quantifiers and formulas without the variable

If xxx does not occur free in ψ\psiψ⁠:

∀x. (ψ⟶φ)≡ψ⟶(∀x. φ)∀x. (φ⟶ψ)≡(∃x. φ)⟶ψ\forall x.\ (\psi \imp \varphi) \equiv \psi \imp (\forall x.\ \varphi) \qquad\qquad \forall x.\ (\varphi \imp \psi) \equiv (\exists x.\ \varphi) \imp \psi ∀x. (ψ⟶φ)≡ψ⟶(∀x. φ)∀x. (φ⟶ψ)≡(∃x. φ)⟶ψ
∃x. (φ∧ψ)≡(∃x. φ)∧ψ∀x. (φ∨ψ)≡(∀x. φ)∨ψ\exists x.\ (\varphi \land \psi) \equiv (\exists x.\ \varphi) \land \psi \qquad\qquad \forall x.\ (\varphi \lor \psi) \equiv (\forall x.\ \varphi) \lor \psi ∃x. (φ∧ψ)≡(∃x. φ)∧ψ∀x. (φ∨ψ)≡(∀x. φ)∨ψ

The second law surprises at first: a ∀ that moves out of the left-hand side of an implication becomes an ∃. An example makes it plausible: “for every user xxx⁠, if xxx is logged in, the server is busy” says the same as “if some user is logged in, the server is busy”. The law is the idea behind the proof rule for ∃ in Modules 6 and 7.

Fact 3.4Order of quantifiers
∀x. ∀y. φ≡∀y. ∀x. φ∃x. ∃y. φ≡∃y. ∃x. φ\forall x.\ \forall y.\ \varphi \equiv \forall y.\ \forall x.\ \varphi \qquad\qquad \exists x.\ \exists y.\ \varphi \equiv \exists y.\ \exists x.\ \varphi ∀x. ∀y. φ≡∀y. ∀x. φ∃x. ∃y. φ≡∃y. ∃x. φ
∃y. ∀x. φ⊨∀x. ∃y. φ, but not conversely.\exists y.\ \forall x.\ \varphi \models \forall x.\ \exists y.\ \varphi, \text{ but not conversely.} ∃y. ∀x. φ⊨∀x. ∃y. φ, but not conversely.

Quantifiers of the same kind can be swapped; ∀ and ∃ cannot. If one yyy works for every xxx⁠, then every xxx has a yyy⁠, namely that one. The converse fails because the yyy may depend on xxx⁠: every lock has a key that opens it, but there need not be a master key that opens every lock. Example 3.4 gave a structure in which ∀x. ∃y. R x y\forall x.\ \exists y.\ R\ x\ y∀x. ∃y. R x y is true and ∃y. ∀x. R x y\exists y.\ \forall x.\ R\ x\ y∃y. ∀x. R x y false.

Substitution and capture

From ∀x. φ\forall x.\ \varphi∀x. φ you may conclude φ\varphiφ for any particular object: from “every admin is on call” follows “if Alice is an admin, Alice is on call”. The step replaces the variable xxx by a term.

Definition 3.10Substitution

φ[t/x]\varphi[t/x]φ[t/x]⁠, read “φ\varphiφ with ttt for xxx”, is the formula obtained from φ\varphiφ by replacing every free occurrence of the variable xxx with the term ttt⁠, renaming bound variables first if necessary so that no free variable of ttt becomes bound. Bound occurrences of xxx are not replaced.

For example, (P x∧(∀x. Q x))[a/x](P\ x \land (\forall x.\ Q\ x))[a/x](P x∧(∀x. Q x))[a/x] is P a∧(∀x. Q x)P\ a \land (\forall x.\ Q\ x)P a∧(∀x. Q x)⁠: only the first xxx is free.

Fact 3.5Instantiation
∀x. φ⊨φ[t/x]φ[t/x]⊨∃x. φ\forall x.\ \varphi \models \varphi[t/x] \qquad\qquad \varphi[t/x] \models \exists x.\ \varphi ∀x. φ⊨φ[t/x]φ[t/x]⊨∃x. φ

provided that no variable of ttt becomes bound when ttt is put in place of xxx⁠.

The proviso is not a technicality. Let φ\varphiφ be ∃y. R x y\exists y.\ R\ x\ y∃y. R x y⁠, “xxx has an RRR-successor”. Then ∀x. φ\forall x.\ \varphi∀x. φ says that everything has a successor. Put yyy for xxx carelessly and you get ∃y. R y y\exists y.\ R\ y\ y∃y. R y y⁠, “something is its own successor”: the yyy you put in has been captured by ∃y\exists y∃y⁠, and the meaning has changed. With the natural numbers as domain and <<< as RRR⁠, ∀x. ∃y. x<y\forall x.\ \exists y.\ x < y∀x. ∃y. x<y is true and ∃y. y<y\exists y.\ y < y∃y. y<y is false, so the careless step leads from a truth to a falsehood. The cure is to rename the bound variable first. ∃y. R x y\exists y.\ R\ x\ y∃y. R x y is equivalent to ∃z. R x z\exists z.\ R\ x\ z∃z. R x z⁠, and (∃z. R x z)[y/x](\exists z.\ R\ x\ z)[y/x](∃z. R x z)[y/x] is ∃z. R y z\exists z.\ R\ y\ z∃z. R y z⁠, “yyy has a successor”, which does follow.

Formulas that differ only in the names of their bound variables, such as ∃y. R x y\exists y.\ R\ x\ y∃y. R x y and ∃z. R x z\exists z.\ R\ x\ z∃z. R x z⁠, are called α-equivalent (alpha-equivalent). They mean the same, and substitution is always understood up to such renaming.

Example 3.8Substitution with renaming

Compute (∀y. R x y⟶P y)[f y/x](\forall y.\ R\ x\ y \imp P\ y)[f\ y/x](∀y. R x y⟶P y)[f y/x]⁠. The term f yf\ yf y contains yyy⁠, which the formula binds, so putting f yf\ yf y in directly would capture its yyy⁠. First rename the bound yyy to a fresh zzz⁠, which gives ∀z. R x z⟶P z\forall z.\ R\ x\ z \imp P\ z∀z. R x z⟶P z⁠, then substitute:

∀z. R (f y) z⟶P z\forall z.\ R\ (f\ y)\ z \imp P\ z∀z. R (f y) z⟶P z

The yyy in f yf\ yf y is still free, as it was in the term you put in.

Equality

Equality is a predicate with a fixed meaning. A structure chooses what PPP and RRR mean, but not what === means: in every structure, t1=t2t_1 = t_2t1​=t2​ is true exactly when t1t_1t1​ and t2t_2t2​ stand for the same element. That is why === counts as a logical symbol. Its laws hold in every structure.

Fact 3.6Laws of equality
  • Reflexivity: t=tt = tt=t⁠.
  • Symmetry: t=u⟶u=tt = u \imp u = tt=u⟶u=t⁠.
  • Transitivity: t=u∧u=v⟶t=vt = u \land u = v \imp t = vt=u∧u=v⟶t=v⁠.
  • Substitution of equals: t=u⟶(φ[t/x]⟶φ[u/x])t = u \imp (\varphi[t/x] \imp \varphi[u/x])t=u⟶(φ[t/x]⟶φ[u/x])⁠.

The last law says that equal objects have the same properties. If owner report=alice\mathit{owner}\ \mathit{report} = \mathit{alice}owner report=alice and admin (owner report)\mathit{admin}\ (\mathit{owner}\ \mathit{report})admin (owner report)⁠, then admin alice\mathit{admin}\ \mathit{alice}admin alice⁠.

With equality you can count. Two variables need not stand for different objects: ∃x y. P x∧P y\exists x\ y.\ P\ x \land P\ y∃x y. P x∧P y is true as soon as a single element has PPP⁠, with xxx and yyy both standing for it. To say “two”, you must say that they differ.

Writing specifications

Turning requirements into formulas is a craft, and a formula is only as good as the translation behind it. A routine helps:

  1. Fix the domain and the vocabulary: which objects, which properties and relations, which functions.
  2. Translate each requirement on its own, using the patterns below.
  3. Read the formula back in plain English, literally, symbol by symbol, and compare it with the requirement.
  4. Test it on small structures: one that should satisfy it and one that should not. The model explorer of Section 3.3 and Nitpick both help.

Types: from many-sorted logic to HOL

The policy of Example 3.10 needed the guards user\mathit{user}user and doc\mathit{doc}doc because one domain held two kinds of object. Guards are tedious, and forgetting one changes the meaning without any warning. Leave the guard out of requirement 3 and it says that admins can read users. Write “every document has an owner” as ∀d. ∃u. owns u d\forall d.\ \exists u.\ \mathit{owns}\ u\ d∀d. ∃u. owns u d and you have claimed that every object, users included, is owned by something. And a function symbol such as owner\mathit{owner}owner must give a value even for a user, because a structure interprets it by a function on the whole domain.

Definition 3.11Many-sorted logic

In a many-sorted or typed logic, the vocabulary gives each constant, each argument of a function or predicate symbol and each function result a sort, also called a type. A structure has one non-empty domain per sort in the convention used here, and each variable ranges over the domain of one sort: ∀d::doc. ∃u::user. owns u d\forall d{::}\mathit{doc}.\ \exists u{::}\mathit{user}.\ \mathit{owns}\ u\ d∀d::doc. ∃u::user. owns u d reads “for every document ddd there is a user uuu who owns ddd”. An expression that puts an argument of the wrong sort somewhere is not a formula at all.

Many-sorted first-order logic can be translated into one-domain first-order logic. Quantifiers become guarded quantifiers: ∀d::doc. φ\forall d{::}\mathit{doc}.\ \varphi∀d::doc. φ becomes ∀d. doc d⟶φ\forall d.\ \mathit{doc}\ d \imp \varphi∀d. doc d⟶φ⁠, and ∃u::user. φ\exists u{::}\mathit{user}.\ \varphi∃u::user. φ becomes ∃u. user u∧φ\exists u.\ \mathit{user}\ u \land \varphi∃u. user u∧φ⁠. The translation also needs assumptions that the sorts are non-empty and that constants and functions respect their declared sorts. Types make such restrictions part of the language, so wrong-sort arguments become errors that a computer finds before proof. This translation concerns first-order sorts; HOL also adds higher-order quantification.

Isabelle/HOL is typed through and through. Every term has a type. Formulas are terms of type bool, whose two values are True and False. A predicate on objects of type τ (tau) is a function of type τ ⇒ bool, which maps each object to True or False, and a relation between τ and σ (sigma) is a function of type τ ⇒ σ ⇒ bool. So P x in Isabelle is a function applied to an argument, and the result is a formula. Because predicates and functions are values like any other, HOL also lets you quantify over them, as in ∀P. P x ⟶ P x: that is what makes it a higher-order logic. Module 8 covers HOL’s types and functions in depth.

To declare a vocabulary in Isabelle, you declare its types with typedecl and its symbols with consts:

Isabelle

  admin :: "user ⇒ bool"
  owns :: "user ⇒ doc ⇒ bool"
  can_delete :: "user ⇒ doc ⇒ bool"
  alice :: user
  report :: doc

typedecl user declares a new type about which nothing is known except that it has at least one element: a domain. consts declares constants with their types but without definitions. They are uninterpreted, exactly like the symbols of a vocabulary: a structure decides what they mean, and Isabelle proves a lemma about them only if it holds whatever they mean. Decidability: what tools can and cannot do

For propositional logic, Module 2 gave an algorithm that decides validity: compute the truth table. It may be slow, but it always finishes with the right answer. Is there such an algorithm for predicate logic? David Hilbert and Wilhelm Ackermann asked this in 1928, as the Entscheidungsproblem, the “decision problem”. The answer came in two parts.

Fact 3.7Gödel’s completeness theorem

There are proof systems for predicate logic, natural deduction among them (Module 6), in which a formula can be proved exactly when it is valid. Kurt Gödel proved this for one such system in his doctoral thesis of 1929, published in 1930.

Completeness has a practical consequence. Proofs are finite texts that a program can check, so a program can list all proofs, shortest first, together with the formulas they prove. Every valid formula appears in the list sooner or later. To find out whether a formula is valid, run the program and wait for the formula to appear: if it is valid, the answer “yes” comes in the end. If it is not, you wait for ever, and at no point do you know whether waiting longer would help.

Fact 3.8Undecidability of predicate logic

No algorithm decides, for every formula of predicate logic, whether it is valid. Alonzo Church and Alan Turing proved this independently in 1936. Validity stays undecidable even when the vocabulary has a single predicate symbol, with two arguments.

Turing’s proof connects to the halting problem that Module 1 mentioned: for every program you can write a formula that is valid exactly when the program halts, so an algorithm for validity would decide halting, which no algorithm can.

Definition 3.12Decidable and semi-decidable

A question about inputs is decidable if some algorithm answers it correctly for every input and always terminates. It is semi-decidable if some algorithm answers “yes” for every input whose answer is yes, but may run for ever on the others.

Validity in predicate logic is semi-decidable but not decidable. The same holds for unsatisfiability, since φ\varphiφ is unsatisfiable exactly when ¬φ\lnot \varphi¬φ is valid; satisfiability is not even semi-decidable.

Two theorems of Gödel are easily confused. The completeness theorem (1929/1930) is about the valid formulas, those true in every structure: all of them can be proved. His incompleteness theorems (1931) concern the formulas true in one particular structure, the natural numbers: no proof system whose axioms a program can check, and which proves only true statements, proves all of them. HOL can describe the natural numbers, so incompleteness applies to Isabelle’s logic too.

Why do some formulas defeat both kinds of search? Because a countermodel can be infinite. Take

(∀x. ¬ R x x)∧(∀x y z. R x y∧R y z⟶R x z)⟶¬(∀x. ∃y. R x y)(\forall x.\ \lnot\, R\ x\ x) \land (\forall x\ y\ z.\ R\ x\ y \land R\ y\ z \imp R\ x\ z) \imp \lnot (\forall x.\ \exists y.\ R\ x\ y) (∀x. ¬R x x)∧(∀x y z. R x y∧R y z⟶R x z)⟶¬(∀x. ∃y. R x y)

Here irreflexive means that no element is related to itself, and transitive means that R x yR\ x\ yR x y and R y zR\ y\ zR y z together imply R x zR\ x\ zR x z⁠. The formula says: “If RRR is irreflexive and transitive, then some element has no RRR-successor.” It is not valid: with the natural numbers as domain and <<< as RRR⁠, both premises hold, and every number has a larger one. But it is true in every finite structure (Exercise 3.8 asks you to show why), so a search through finite structures never finds a countermodel, and a proof search never finds a proof, because there is none. Such formulas must exist. If every formula that is not valid had a finite countermodel, you could run a proof search and a countermodel search side by side; one of them would always finish, and validity would be decidable, which Church and Turing showed it is not.

What this means in practice:

  • Proof search (simp, blast, auto, and the provers that Sledgehammer calls in Module 9) can close a goal, leave subgoals, fail or reach a resource limit. A completed proof gives a theorem; an unfinished attempt does not establish that the claim is false.
  • Countermodel search (Nitpick and Quickcheck) is the other half: a genuine counterexample shows that a formula is not valid; finding none shows nothing.
  • When both give up, the work is yours: find the idea of the proof and guide Isabelle through it, as Parts II and III teach, or find a countermodel that the tools cannot see.

Not everything is undecidable. Propositional logic is decidable, and so is the part of predicate logic in which every predicate takes one argument and there are no function symbols. SAT and SMT solvers (Module 1) can solve many useful instances of decidable fragments efficiently, although no practical running time is guaranteed for every instance. That is why Isabelle’s tools call them for help.

Key idea

Validity in predicate logic is semi-decidable: a systematic search finds a proof whenever one exists, but no program can always tell you that none exists. That is why Isabelle offers both provers and counterexample finders, why automation sometimes gives up on true statements, and why the rest of this series teaches you to guide proofs yourself.

What you learned

  • Predicate logic adds terms, which name objects, predicates, which state properties and relations, and the quantifiers ∀ (“for all”) and ∃ (“there exists”) to propositional logic.
  • In Isabelle a quantifier’s scope extends as far to the right as possible. Occurrences of a variable in the scope of its quantifier are bound, the others free; a sentence has no free variables.
  • A structure is a non-empty domain with an interpretation of the vocabulary. A false ∀ has a counterexample, a true ∃ a witness.
  • A formula is valid if it is true in every structure, satisfiable if it is true in some. One countermodel refutes validity; validity needs an argument about every structure.
  • Negation swaps ∀ and ∃; ∀ distributes over ∧ and ∃ over ∨, but not the other way round; ∃y. ∀x implies ∀x. ∃y, but not conversely. Substitution must not capture variables.
  • For restricted statements such as “every admin is on call”, use ∀ with ⟶; for “some admin is on call”, use ∃ with ∧. These are translation patterns, not restrictions on which connectives may occur under a quantifier. “Only” introduces a necessary condition, and counting uses equality.
  • In Isabelle, predicates are functions to bool; typedecl and consts declare a vocabulary; free variables of a lemma are implicitly universal; blast proves first-order goals and nitpick finds countermodels. = binds more tightly than ¬ and ∧, ⟷ as loosely as ⟶.
  • Validity is semi-decidable (Gödel’s completeness theorem) but undecidable (Church and Turing, 1936): provers may give up on valid formulas, and countermodel finders miss infinite countermodels.

Pick any element x0x_0x0​⁠, then x1x_1x1​ with R x0 x1R\ x_0\ x_1R x0​ x1​⁠, then x2x_2x2​ with R x1 x2R\ x_1\ x_2R x1​ x2​⁠, and so on.
