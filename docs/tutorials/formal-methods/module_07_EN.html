Two logics in one system

In Module 6 you built natural-deduction proofs on paper. You started from the formula you wanted, chose the rule that could have produced it, and worked upwards until every branch ended in an assumption. Isabelle builds proofs in exactly this way, and checks every step. In this module you redo the proofs of Module 6 in Isabelle, rule by rule, so that each paper tree becomes a short proof script.

Before the first proof you need to read the notation Isabelle uses for rules and goals. It involves a second, smaller logic underneath HOL.

Pure, the meta-logic

Module 1 described Isabelle as a framework in which many logics can be defined. The framework has a small logic of its own, Isabelle/Pure, or Pure for short. Its only job is to say what rules and proofs are. Every other logic, HOL included, is defined inside Pure.

Definition 7.1Meta-logic and object logic

A meta-logic is a logic for stating rules and proofs about another logic, the object logic. In Isabelle the meta-logic is Pure and the object logic is HOL. Pure has three symbols of its own: ⟹ (“from … infer”), ⋀ (“for an arbitrary …”) and ≡, Pure’s own equality, which Isabelle uses above all to state definitions.

Each Pure symbol has a HOL counterpart that you already know from Modules 2 and 3:

Pure Read it as HOL Read it as
A ⟹ B from A, infer B A ⟶ B A implies B
⋀x. P x for an arbitrary x, P x ∀x. P x for all x, P x
a ≡ b a equals b; in a definition, a is defined as b a = b a equals b

In Isabelle/jEdit you type ⟹ as ==>, ⋀ as !! and ≡ as ==. The brackets ⟦ and ⟧, which appear below, are [| and |].

Why does Isabelle need two arrows? Because a rule of natural deduction is not a formula. The rule (∧I) of Module 6 does not say “A ∧ B is true”. It says “if you have derived A and you have derived B, you may write down A ∧ B”. It is a statement about derivations, which Module 6 drew as a horizontal line. Pure gives Isabelle a language for statements of this kind. In Pure, (∧I) reads

⟦A; B⟧⟹A∧B\llbracket A;\ B \rrbracket \Imp A \land B [[A; B]]⟹A∧B

“from A and B, infer A ∧ B”. The formulas A, B and A ∧ B belong to HOL; the brackets, the semicolon and ⟹ belong to Pure. HOL formulas have the type bool. Pure statements have a type of their own, prop, and Isabelle turns each HOL formula into a Pure statement where one is needed, without showing you.

Fact 7.1Rules with several premises

A₁ ⟹ A₂ ⟹ … ⟹ Aₙ ⟹ C means A₁ ⟹ (A₂ ⟹ … ⟹ (Aₙ ⟹ C)), and Isabelle prints it as ⟦A₁; A₂; …; Aₙ⟧ ⟹ C: from the premises A₁ to Aₙ, infer the conclusion C. A premise can itself be a rule: in (A ⟹ B) ⟹ C, the premise A ⟹ B is a derivation of B that may assume A.

Goals use the same arrow

Isabelle writes goals with ⟹ too. Compare two lemmas:

Isabelle
lemma "A ⟹ A ∨ B"
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ⟹ A ∨ B

This goal is a rule: from A, infer A ∨ B. A is an assumption you may use, and A ∨ B is the conclusion you must reach. blast, from Module 2, proves it:

Isabelle
  by blast
Isabelle
lemma "A ⟶ A ∨ B"
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ⟶ A ∨ B

Here the whole goal is one HOL formula, and there are no assumptions yet. It says the same thing, and blast proves it too:

Isabelle
  by blast

The difference lies in how you work with the two forms. On paper, the first step of a proof of A ⟶ A ∨ B is (⟶I): assume A, then prove A ∨ B. In Isabelle that step turns the ⟶ of the goal into ⟹, after which the goal looks exactly like the first lemma. You will take this step in Section 7.3.

The manual Programming and Proving in Isabelle/HOL gives a practical rule: state a lemma as ⟦A₁; …; Aₙ⟧ ⟹ C rather than A₁ ∧ … ∧ Aₙ ⟶ C when you want to use it as a rule later, because Isabelle can apply the first form directly. In this module the lemmas keep the ⟶ form of Module 6, so that each Isabelle proof follows its paper tree step by step.

Definitions with ≡

The third symbol of Pure, ≡, appears in definitions. Module 6 explained ¬A as A ⟶ ⊥. HOL defines negation in the same way, as the command thm shows (it displays a stored theorem by its name; Section 7.2 uses it all the time):

Isabelle
thm not_def
Output
¬ ?P ≡ ?P ⟶ False

Read it as “¬P is defined as P ⟶ False”. Isabelle writes ⊥ as False. The question marks are explained in Section 7.2.

Watch out

⟹ belongs to Pure. It joins statements, but it can never appear inside a HOL formula:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "(A ⟹ B) ∧ C"
Output
Inner syntax error⌂
at "∧ C"
Failed to parse prop

Isabelle read (A ⟹ B) as a Pure statement and then could not go on with ∧ C, because ∧ joins HOL formulas. Inside a formula, write ⟶. Note also that ⟹ binds more weakly than every symbol of HOL: A ∧ B ⟹ C means (A ∧ B) ⟹ C.

Rules are theorems

Look a rule up with thm

In Isabelle a rule is not a separate kind of object. It is a theorem whose statement uses ⟹, and thm shows it:

Isabelle
thm conjI
Output
⟦?P; ?Q⟧ ⟹ ?P ∧ ?Q

Compare it with (∧I) as Module 6 drew it:

P
Q
∧I
P ∧ Q

The premises above the line become the premises before ⟹, and the conclusion below the line becomes the conclusion after it. The name, conjI, is short for conjunction introduction.

Definition 7.2Schematic variable

A schematic variable, or unknown, is a variable written with a question mark, such as ?P. It is a placeholder: when a theorem is used, Isabelle may replace it with any term of the right type, the same term at every occurrence. When Isabelle stores a proved lemma, it turns the lemma’s free variables into schematic variables, which records that the lemma holds for all their values.

So conjI works for any two formulas. You can choose them yourself with of, which replaces the schematic variables from left to right:

Isabelle
thm conjI[of "door_closed" "lift_stopped"]
Output
⟦door_closed; lift_stopped⟧ ⟹ door_closed ∧ lift_stopped

Here door_closed and lift_stopped are free variables of type bool: two facts about a lift, say. Usually you do not choose by hand. Isabelle finds the right replacement itself, by unification (Section 7.3).

A few things to notice:

  • The names follow a pattern: I for introduction, E for elimination, D for destruction (explained below), and 1 and 2 for the two versions of a rule. mp is modus ponens, spec is specialisation, refl is reflexivity.
  • ⊥ is False, and Isabelle prints ¬ followed by a space: ¬ ?P.
  • notE concludes any formula ?R. With ?R taken to be False it is (¬E), which derives ⊥ from ¬P and P. With any other ?R it is (¬E) followed by (⊥E), which derives anything from ⊥.
  • ccontr is (RAA) exactly: if assuming ¬P leads to ⊥, then P. Section 7.7 explains classical.
  • On formulas, Isabelle prints ⟷ as = (Module 3), so ?P = ?Q in iffI is ?P ⟷ ?Q. Module 6’s list has no rules for ⟷: iffI proves an equivalence from the two implications, and iffD1 and iffD2 use one in either direction.

Three shapes of rule

Most rules in the table come in one of three shapes. The shape decides how you use a rule (Sections 7.3 and 7.4), so it is worth learning to see it.

Definition 7.3Introduction, elimination and destruction rules
  • An introduction rule has the connective in its conclusion: conjI, disjI1, disjI2, impI, notI, allI, exI, iffI.
  • An elimination rule has the connective (or False) in its first premise, the major premise, and an arbitrary formula, a schematic variable such as ?R, as its conclusion. Its other premises say what you may assume while you prove that formula: conjE, disjE, impE, notE, FalseE, allE, exE.
  • A destruction rule has the connective in its major premise and a part or instance of the major premise as its conclusion: conjunct1, conjunct2, mp, spec, iffD1, iffD2.

Isabelle often offers two versions of one paper rule: a destruction rule that reads like the paper rule (conjunct1, mp, spec), and an elimination rule that does the same work in a form better suited to reasoning about assumptions (conjE, impE, allE). Section 7.4 shows why the second form helps.

The rules that discharge assumptions or need an eigenvariable deserve a closer look, because Pure states their side conditions without any words.

Fact 7.2How Pure writes discharge and eigenvariables
  • In impI, (?P ⟹ ?Q) ⟹ ?P ⟶ ?Q, the premise ?P ⟹ ?Q is a derivation of ?Q that may use the assumption ?P: the [A]¹ of Module 6. The assumption is available exactly inside that premise, so no discharge numbers are needed.
  • In disjE, the premises ?P ⟹ ?R and ?Q ⟹ ?R are the two cases, each with its own assumption.
  • In allI, (⋀x. ?P x) ⟹ ∀x. ?P x, the premise says “?P x for an arbitrary x”. The variable x is local to the premise, so it cannot occur in any assumption from outside. This is the eigenvariable condition of (∀I).
  • In exE, ⟦∃x. ?P x; ⋀x. ?P x ⟹ ?Q⟧ ⟹ ?Q, the second premise says “for an arbitrary x with ?P x, derive ?Q”. ?Q stands outside the scope of ⋀x, so it cannot mention x. This is the condition of (∃E) that the eigenvariable must not occur in the conclusion.

Proving backwards: rule and assumption

The plan

Take the first theorem that Module 6 derived with natural deduction, A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A (Section 6.4). Here is its tree:

[A ∧ B]¹
∧E₂
B
[A ∧ B]¹
∧E₁
A
∧I
B ∧ A
⟶I¹

Read it from the bottom up, the way you found it. The formula is an implication, so the last step is (⟶I): assume A ∧ B and prove B ∧ A. That is a conjunction, so the step above is (∧I), which leaves B and A. Each of them comes out of the assumption, by (∧E₂) and (∧E₁). In Isabelle you take the same steps in the same order. Each step names a rule; Isabelle applies it to the goal and shows you what is left.

Applying a rule

State the lemma. The name swap_and lets you refer to it later:

Isabelle
lemma swap_and: "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A

proof (prove) means that Isabelle is waiting for proof steps. Below it are the subgoals still to be proved, numbered. At the start there is one: the whole statement.

Definition 7.4Subgoal and proof state

A subgoal has the form ⋀x₁ … xₖ. ⟦H₁; …; Hₙ⟧ ⟹ C: for arbitrary values of the variables x₁ … xₖ (Section 7.6), and under the assumptions H₁ … Hₙ, prove the conclusion C. The variables and the assumptions may be missing. The proof state is the list of subgoals. The proof is complete when the list is empty.

A subgoal is the goal Γ ⊢ C of Module 6’s backward search (Section 6.7), written in Pure: the assumptions Γ go before ⟹, and the conclusion C after it.

Definition 7.5The method rule

apply (rule r) applies the rule r backwards to subgoal 1. It unifies the conclusion of r with the conclusion of the subgoal (see “Unification” below), and replaces the subgoal with one new subgoal for each premise of r. Each new subgoal keeps the assumptions of the old one; a premise of the form P ⟹ Q adds P to them.

The first step is (⟶I):

Isabelle
  apply (rule impI)
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ∧ B ⟹ B ∧ A

impI is (?P ⟹ ?Q) ⟹ ?P ⟶ ?Q. Its conclusion ?P ⟶ ?Q matches the goal with ?P = A ∧ B and ?Q = B ∧ A, so the new subgoal is the premise, A ∧ B ⟹ B ∧ A. A ∧ B is now an assumption. It is available in this subgoal and in every subgoal that grows out of it, exactly like the open assumption [A ∧ B]¹ in the tree.

Next, (∧I):

Isabelle
  apply (rule conjI)
Output
proof (prove)
goal (2 subgoals):
 1. A ∧ B ⟹ B
 2. A ∧ B ⟹ A

conjI has two premises, so there are now two subgoals, one for each branch of the tree. Both keep the assumption A ∧ B. The rule and assumption steps used here act on subgoal 1; subgoal 2 waits its turn. Other methods, such as auto and simp_all, can work on all subgoals (Module 5).

Now (∧E₂), used backwards:

Isabelle
  apply (rule conjunct2)
Output
proof (prove)
goal (2 subgoals):
 1. A ∧ B ⟹ ?P5 ∧ B
 2. A ∧ B ⟹ A

conjunct2 is ?P ∧ ?Q ⟹ ?Q. Its conclusion ?Q matches B, so its premise becomes a conjunction whose right half is B and whose left half is not yet known. Isabelle keeps that left half as a schematic variable in the new subgoal, ?P5: the variable ?P with the number 5 attached, its index, so that it cannot clash with other variables (the index has no other meaning). On paper you would look at the assumptions and write A ∧ B at once. Isabelle postpones the choice until a later step settles it.

Closing a subgoal with an assumption

Definition 7.6The method assumption

apply assumption proves subgoal 1 if its conclusion unifies with one of its assumptions. Schematic variables in the conclusion are instantiated as needed, everywhere in the proof state.

Isabelle

Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ∧ B ⟹ A

Unifying ?P5 ∧ B with the assumption A ∧ B sets ?P5 to A, and the subgoal is closed: that is the leaf [A ∧ B]¹ of the left branch. The right branch goes the same way with conjunct1, whose premise becomes a conjunction with A on the left:

No subgoals!

No subgoals are left. done ends the proof:

Definition 7.7The command done

done finishes a proof when no subgoals are left. It does not prove anything itself: if any subgoal remains, done fails (Section 7.8).

Isabelle has now stored the lemma under its name:

Isabelle
thm swap_and
Output
?A ∧ ?B ⟶ ?B ∧ ?A

The free variables A and B have become schematic. swap_and holds for any two formulas, and you can use it as a rule, like conjI. Figure 7.2 puts the tree and the script side by side.

Think of it like this

The proof state is a to-do list. In these rule-by-rule scripts, apply replaces the first item with the subgoals of the chosen step; done checks that the list is empty.

Unification

Definition 7.8Unification

Unification makes two terms identical by choosing values for their schematic variables. Isabelle uses it whenever it applies a rule: in rule, between the conclusion of the rule and the conclusion of the subgoal; in assumption, between the conclusion of the subgoal and an assumption. If no choice of values makes the two terms identical, that application cannot succeed. Isabelle also respects types and variable scopes, and compares terms modulo bound-variable renaming and lambda conversion; it does not prove arbitrary arithmetic identities while unifying. Higher-order unification is not a general decision procedure, so a search need not find every possible instantiation.

Unify with Result
?P ⟶ ?Q A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A ?P = A ∧ B and ?Q = B ∧ A
?P5 ∧ B A ∧ B ?P5 = A
?P ∧ ?Q A ∧ (B ∨ C) ?P = A and ?Q = B ∨ C
?P ∨ ?Q B ∧ A impossible: ∨ is not ∧
A ∧ ?Q B ∧ A impossible: A is not B

The last row matters. A and B in the goal are not schematic: they are free variables that stand for fixed but arbitrary formulas, and unification never replaces them. That is why the proof works for every A and B. It never used anything about them.

Key idea

Proving in Isabelle is building the paper tree from the root upwards. rule adds an inference on top of an open branch, assumption closes a branch at an open assumption, and schematic variables are the parts of the tree you have not filled in yet.

4

Using assumptions: erule, drule and frule

In Section 7.3 the conjunction A ∧ B sat among the assumptions, and you reached its parts by working backwards from B and from A, leaving Isabelle to guess ?P5 on the way. On paper you think the other way round: you have A ∧ B, so you take it apart. Elimination and destruction rules are made for that, and Isabelle has three methods that apply them to assumptions.

erule: take an assumption apart

Definition 7.9The method erule

apply (erule r) applies an elimination rule r to an assumption of subgoal 1. It unifies the major premise of r with an assumption, and the conclusion of r with the conclusion of the subgoal. It removes that assumption and adds one new subgoal for each of the other premises of r.

With conjE, ⟦?P ∧ ?Q; ⟦?P; ?Q⟧ ⟹ ?R⟧ ⟹ ?R, the effect is to replace an assumption P ∧ Q with the two assumptions P and Q. The swap proof becomes shorter:

Isabelle
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
  apply (rule impI)
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ∧ B ⟹ B ∧ A
Isabelle
  apply (erule conjE)
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. ⟦A; B⟧ ⟹ B ∧ A
Isabelle
  apply (rule conjI)
Output
proof (prove)
goal (2 subgoals):
 1. ⟦A; B⟧ ⟹ B
 2. ⟦A; B⟧ ⟹ A
Isabelle
  apply assumption
  apply assumption
  done

After erule conjE, A and B are separate assumptions, so each half of B ∧ A is closed by assumption with nothing to guess. This is the usual way to use a conjunction you have.

drule and frule: reason forwards

Definition 7.10The methods drule and frule

apply (drule r) applies a destruction rule r forwards. It unifies the major premise of r with an assumption of subgoal 1 and removes that assumption. Each other premise of r becomes a new subgoal, and then the original subgoal comes back with the conclusion of r as a new assumption. apply (frule r) does the same but keeps the assumption it used.

Modus ponens is the typical destruction rule. Module 6 proved (A ⟶ B) ⟶ (B ⟶ C) ⟶ (A ⟶ C) with three (⟶I) steps and two (⟶E) steps:

intro applied impI and then conjI, which split the goal; elim took the conjunction apart in subgoal 1 only. In imp_chain above, apply (intro impI) would replace the three rule impI steps.

Which method when

Situation Kind of rule Method Example
The conclusion has a connective to break up introduction rule apply (rule conjI)
An assumption has a connective to take apart elimination erule apply (erule disjE)
An assumption gives a new fact destruction drule, or frule to keep it apply (drule mp)
The conclusion is one of the assumptions none assumption apply assumption
Many steps of one kind introduction or elimination intro, elim apply (intro impI conjI)

These are customary pairings, not restrictions enforced by the method names. rule can use an elimination or destruction theorem backwards too, as rule conjunct2 already did. Likewise, erule can use a theorem such as mp when its first premise and conclusion fit. The methods differ in how they resolve the theorem against the subgoal and its assumptions.

Tip

Look at the subgoal before you look for a rule. If you want to attack the main connective of the conclusion, apply its introduction rule with rule. If you want to use an assumption, apply the elimination rule of its connective with erule. Section 7.8 shows what happens when the two are mixed up.

Negation and falsity

Section 7.1 showed that HOL defines ¬A as A ⟶ False, in the same way as Module 6. The negation rules follow from that definition:

exI, ?P ?x ⟹ ∃x. ?P x, is (∃I): to prove ∃x. P x, prove P t for some term t, the witness. Key idea

Fix first, guess later. rule allI and erule exE fix variables; rule exI, erule allE and drule spec create unknowns. An unknown can only become a term built from variables that were fixed before it was created, as recorded by its arguments in the displayed subgoal. Context parameters and constants remain available too. Fixing variables first is a useful strategy for these proofs, rather than a requirement on every possible HOL proof. This is the advice of Section 6.7, to open existential assumptions first and then choose instances and witnesses, and Isabelle’s unknowns show why it works.

For a statement that is not valid, no order works. The converse, (∀y. ∃x. R x y) ⟶ (∃x. ∀y. R x y), says that if every door has a key, there is a master key. Section 6.6 gave a countermodel, and Exercise 7.9 lets you watch the attempt get stuck.

Equality

refl, ?t = ?t, is (=I), and subst, ⟦?s = ?t; ?P ?s⟧ ⟹ ?P ?t, is (=E): if s = t and some property holds of s, it holds of t. The property ?P is a schematic variable of function type, which unification fills in with a function. Module 6 derived symmetry from these two rules (Section 6.8), with x = s as P x. Written as a rule, from s = t to t = s, the derivation is:

s = t
=I
s = s
=E
t = s

Here (=E) uses the property “equal to s”: it holds of s, so it holds of t.

Isabelle
lemma "s = t ⟹ t = s"
  apply (erule subst)
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. s = s

erule unified the major premise ?s = ?t with the assumption s = t, and the conclusion ?P t with t = s. For the second, Isabelle had to find a property ?P such that ?P t is t = s. The new subgoal shows which one it chose: the property “equal to s”, since the remaining premise ?P s has become s = s. refl proves it:

Isabelle
  apply (rule refl)
  done

In everyday proofs you rarely use subst by hand, because the simplifier rewrites with equations automatically (Module 9). But subst is what such rewriting means in natural deduction.

When a step fails

Any step can fail, and Isabelle’s messages are short. This section collects the ones you will meet, with their causes and fixes. Isabelle marks the step that failed and shows the proof state it was given. The steps before it are not affected, so you can replace the failing step with another.

Failed to apply proof method

This is the most common message. It means that the method could do nothing with subgoal 1: for rule, the conclusion of the rule does not unify with the conclusion of the subgoal; for erule, either that or no assumption fits the rule’s major premise; for drule and frule, no assumption fits the major premise; for assumption, no assumption unifies with the conclusion. Isabelle shows the proof state it was given. Here is the early choice of a side that Module 6 warned about (Section 6.4), followed by a hopeful assumption:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "A ∨ ¬ A"
  apply (rule disjI1)
  apply assumption
  done
Output
Failed to apply proof method⌂:
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A

disjI1 left the subgoal A, which has no assumptions at all, so assumption has nothing to unify with. Three other causes are common. Each gives the same message, followed by the proof state as it was before the failing step.

Taking an assumption apart before it is one. In the goal A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A, the conjunction A ∧ B is not an assumption; it is part of the implication, so erule conjE finds nothing to work on:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
  apply (erule conjE)
  done
Output
Failed to apply proof method⌂:
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A

Apply impI first: it moves A ∧ B into the assumptions.

Expecting assumption to look inside a formula. After impI and conjI, subgoal 1 is A ∧ B ⟹ B. B is part of the assumption, but it is not the assumption, so assumption fails:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
  apply (rule impI)
  apply (rule conjI)
  apply assumption
  done
Output
Failed to apply proof method⌂:
goal (2 subgoals):
 1. A ∧ B ⟹ B
 2. A ∧ B ⟹ A

assumption only unifies whole formulas. Take the assumption apart first, with erule conjE.

Using a rule for the wrong connective. disjI1 concludes a disjunction, but the conclusion of the subgoal A ∧ B ⟹ B ∧ A is a conjunction:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
  apply (rule impI)
  apply (rule disjI1)
  done
Output
Failed to apply proof method⌂:
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ∧ B ⟹ B ∧ A

When in doubt, look the rule up with thm: for rule, its conclusion must unify with the conclusion of the subgoal. A fixed connective must match, but a schematic conclusion such as ?P can match different shapes. The rule need not display the goal’s connective before instantiation.

Undefined fact

A misspelt rule name gives a different message. Many rule names end in a capital I, which looks much like a lower-case l:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
  apply (rule impI)
  apply (rule conjl)
  done
Output
Undefined fact: "conjl"⌂

Failed to finish proof

done fails if subgoals remain, and lists them. Take the same early choice as above and write done straight after it:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "A ∨ ¬ A"
  apply (rule disjI1)
  done
Output
Failed to finish proof:
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A

done never proves anything itself, not even a subgoal whose conclusion is one of its assumptions. Add the missing steps or, if the subgoal left is unprovable, as here, go back and choose differently. (A one-line proof by m, as in by blast, behaves a little differently: after the method m, it closes the subgoals that are left by assumption, and reports Failed to finish proof only if something else remains.)

Forgetting done

Without done, the proof is still open when the next command arrives, and the next lemma is refused:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "A ⟶ A"
  apply (rule impI)
  apply assumption

lemma "B ⟶ B"
Output
Bad context for command "lemma"⌂

A lemma cannot start inside a proof. Add done after the last apply. If the unfinished proof is the last one in the theory, end is refused instead, with the message “Illegal application of proof command in “prove” mode”.

A detour, not an error: rule instead of erule

Some mistakes produce no error at all, only a strange proof state. Applying an elimination rule with rule instead of erule is the usual one:

Isabelle
lemma "A ∨ B ⟶ B ∨ A"
  apply (rule impI)
  apply (rule disjE)
Output
proof (prove)
goal (3 subgoals):
 1. A ∨ B ⟹ ?P2 ∨ ?Q2
 2. ⟦A ∨ B; ?P2⟧ ⟹ B ∨ A
 3. ⟦A ∨ B; ?Q2⟧ ⟹ B ∨ A

rule unified only the conclusion ?R of disjE with B ∨ A. The major premise became subgoal 1, so Isabelle asks you to prove some disjunction it does not know yet, and the two cases speak of unknown formulas. apply assumption would repair this by unifying the unknown disjunction with A ∨ B, and the result would be the erule version with the assumption A ∨ B kept in both cases. Use erule from the start.

  oops
If something went wrong
  • Failed to apply proof method: the rule does not fit subgoal 1, or no assumption fits its major premise. Check the main connective; use impI before taking an assumption apart; read the rule with thm.
  • Undefined fact: a misspelt rule name, such as conjl for conjI.
  • Failed to finish proof: subgoals remain at done; they are listed.
  • Bad context for command “lemma”: the previous proof has no done.
  • Inner syntax error … Failed to parse prop: a mistake in the statement itself, such as ⟹ inside a formula (Section 7.1).
  • A subgoal that cannot be closed, with unknowns such as ?x4 y: a quantifier rule came too early. Fix variables before you create unknowns (Section 7.6).

Letting Isabelle search: blast and auto

Module 6 ended by letting blast prove its derivations (Section 6.9). You have now done by hand what blast does automatically. blast belongs to Isabelle’s classical reasoner, a family of proof methods built on natural-deduction rules. It applies introduction and elimination rules like the ones in this module: the safe ones of Section 6.7 freely, the others as choices. When a choice leads nowhere, it can go back and try another, within its search bounds. It may find a complete proof, fail, or hit a resource limit. The examples below take a single call:

Isabelle
lemma "¬ (A ∧ B) ⟶ ¬ A ∨ ¬ B"
  by blast

lemma "(∃x. ∀y. R x y) ⟶ (∀y. ∃x. R x y)"
  by blast

lemma "⟦∀x. safe x ⟶ safe (step x); safe s⟧ ⟹ safe (step (step s))"
  by blast

blast found the instance step s that unification alone got wrong in Section 7.6: when one choice fails, it tries another. And whatever blast finds, the theorem is built by Isabelle’s kernel from the rules of the logic. As Module 1 explains, this guarantee concerns ordinary reconstructed proofs, relative to the theory’s axioms and trusted infrastructure; admissions and oracle methods introduce different evidence.

When the statement is not valid, blast fails, as Section 6.9 showed for the master key:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "(∀y. ∃x. R x y) ⟶ (∃x. ∀y. R x y)"
  by blast
Output
Failed to apply initial proof method⌂:
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. (∀y. ∃x. R x y) ⟶ (∃x. ∀y. R x y)

With by, Isabelle calls the method the initial proof method, so the message says only that blast found no proof. It does not say whether the statement is false or too hard for blast; nitpick (Modules 2 and 3) can look for a counterexample. auto behaves differently: it does what it can and shows you what is left.

Isabelle
lemma "(∀y. ∃x. R x y) ⟶ (∃x. ∀y. R x y)"
  apply auto
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. ∀y. ∃x. R x y ⟹ ∃x. ∀y. R x y
Isabelle
  oops

auto turned the implication into an assumption and stopped there. auto combines classical reasoning with the simplifier, and leaves the subgoals it cannot prove; Module 9 covers it in depth. blast does not invoke the simplifier and knows little about equality, but Programming and Proving in Isabelle/HOL calls it “the method of choice for complex logical goals”.

So which should you use? For routine logic, blast or auto. But automation fails often enough, on goals that are too big or not quite true, and then the manual steps are how you find out why: apply a few rules by hand, look at the subgoals, and call blast on the pieces, or discover that one of them cannot be proved. That is why this module did everything by hand first.

Tip

A good routine when blast fails: apply the obvious introduction rules with intro (impI, allI, conjI, …) and take apart the obvious assumptions with elim (conjE, exE, …). Then try blast on each subgoal. A subgoal that looks false is a hint that the whole statement may be false; try nitpick on the lemma.

What you learned

  • Isabelle has two levels of logic: the meta-logic Pure, with ⟹, ⋀ and ≡, in which rules and goals are written, and the object logic HOL, with ¬ ∧ ∨ ⟶ ∀ ∃ and =, in which formulas are written.
  • A rule is a theorem: thm conjI shows ⟦?P; ?Q⟧ ⟹ ?P ∧ ?Q. Schematic variables such as ?P can be instantiated by terms of the appropriate type and permitted variable dependencies: by unification automatically, or by hand with of and rule_tac.
  • Each rule of Module 6 has an Isabelle name: conjI, conjunct1, conjunct2, conjE, disjI1, disjI2, disjE, impI, mp, impE, notI, notE, FalseE, allI, spec, allE, exI, exE, ccontr, classical, refl and subst.
  • A proof script builds the paper tree from the root: rule matches a rule’s conclusion against the goal; erule, drule and frule resolve rules against assumptions in different ways; assumption closes a branch; intro and elim repeat; done checks that nothing is left.
  • allI and exE create fixed variables (⋀x), which enforce the eigenvariable conditions; exI, allE and spec create unknowns. Fix first, guess later.
  • Classical proofs use ccontr, which is (RAA), or classical.
  • The common messages are Failed to apply proof method, Undefined fact, Failed to finish proof and Bad context for command.
  • blast searches with the same rules and either succeeds or fails; auto shows what it could not prove.

Section 6.7 showed that a backward search for (∀y. ∃x. R x y) ⟶ (∃x. ∀y. R x y) never closes. Watch it happen in Isabelle: apply the quantifier rules in the best order you can find, and show the state where you get stuck. Then explain, with fixed variables and unknowns, why no order can work.

Show solution

Direct introduction of the conclusion’s ∃x uses exI, and it creates the unknown witness before anything is fixed:

Isabelle

To close the subgoal, R x (?y6 y) and R ?x2 y must become identical. The instance ?y6 y can become y, because allI fixed y before allE created ?y6. But the witness ?x2 would have to become x, which exE fixed after exI created ?x2, and that is not allowed. Taking the assumption apart first only moves the problem: erule allE and erule exE then fix x before the witness is created, so the witness can become x, but the instance of ∀y is created before allI fixes y, so it cannot become y. In the subgoal ⟦A ⟶ B; A⟧ ⟹ B, what does ⟹ say?
⟹ is Pure’s “from … infer”. Left of it are the assumptions of the subgoal, right of it the conclusion still to be proved.
2
thm exI prints ?P ?x ⟹ ∃x. ?P x. What does the question mark in ?x mean?
Schematic variables are placeholders constrained by types and scope. Isabelle fills them in by unification, or you choose them with of or rule_tac; a later local variable cannot escape through an earlier unknown.
3
The subgoal is A ∨ B ⟹ B ∨ A. Which step performs (∨E) on the assumption, leaving one subgoal per case?
erule matches the major premise A ∨ B with the assumption and leaves the two cases. rule disjE leaves three subgoals with unknown formulas.
4
After apply (rule allI) the subgoal reads ⋀x. ∀x. P x ∧ Q x ⟹ P x. What is the x after ⋀?
allI turns the HOL quantifier into Pure’s ⋀. The fixed variable is what Module 6 called the eigenvariable.
5
A proof is stuck at ⋀y x. R x (?y8 y x) ⟹ R (?x4 y) y, and apply assumption fails. Why?
The statement is valid (it is ex_all), but exI came before exE. Fix first, guess later.
6
by blast reports “Failed to apply initial proof method”. What do you know?
blast either finds a complete proof or fails. A failure says nothing about truth; use nitpick to look for a counterexample, or apply some rules by hand.
