#! Modules 14 to 16

## sequence
links: 14.1#sequences
An infinite list of numbers in a definite order, $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$, written $(a_n)$: precisely, a function whose domain is the whole numbers from some first index on. $a_n$ is its $n$th term, and $n$ its index.

## term
links: 14.1#sequences 14.3#partial-sums
One of the numbers $a_n$ of a sequence, or one of the numbers added in a series.

## limit of a sequence
links: 14.1#limit
$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L$ means: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a number $N$ such that $\abs{a_n - L} < \varepsilon$ for every $n > N$. Then the sequence converges to $L$, written $a_n \to L$; otherwise it diverges.

## converge
links: 12.8#infinite-intervals 14.1#limit 14.3#partial-sums
An improper integral converges if its defining limit exists as a finite number. A sequence converges if it has a limit $L$, a number. A series converges if its sequence of partial sums converges. Whatever does not converge diverges.

## diverge
links: 12.8#infinite-intervals 14.1#limit 14.3#partial-sums
To fail to converge. A sequence diverges to infinity, $a_n \to \infty$, if for every number $M$ there is an $N$ with $a_n > M$ for every $n > N$.

## geometric sequence
links: 14.1#laws
The sequence $r^n$, $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, for a constant $r$: it converges to 0 if $-1 < r < 1$ and to 1 if $r = 1$, diverges to infinity if $r > 1$, and has no limit if $r \le -1$.

## monotone
links: 14.2#monotone
A sequence is monotone if it is increasing ($a_n \le a_{n+1}$ for every $n$) or decreasing ($a_n \ge a_{n+1}$ for every $n$).

## bounded
links: 14.2#monotone 18.3#absolute-extrema
A sequence is bounded above if some number $M$, an upper bound, has $a_n \le M$ for every $n$; bounded below if some lower bound $m$ has $a_n \ge m$; and bounded if both. A set in the plane is bounded if it lies inside some disc.

## upper bound
links: 14.2#monotone 15.1#convergence
A number $M$ with $a_n \le M$ for every term of a sequence, or $x \le M$ for every element of a set; a lower bound $m$ has $a_n \ge m$. The least upper bound is the smallest upper bound.

## Monotone Convergence Theorem
links: 14.2#mct
An increasing sequence that is bounded above converges, and its limit is at least every term and at most every upper bound; likewise a decreasing sequence that is bounded below. It is a form of the completeness of the real numbers.

## least upper bound property
links: 14.2#mct
Every non-empty set of real numbers that is bounded above has a least upper bound: an upper bound that is at most every other upper bound. Analysis states the completeness of the real numbers in this form.

## recursive sequence
links: 14.2#recursive
A sequence defined by its first term and a fixed rule $a_{n+1} = g(a_n)$ that computes each further term from the one before.

## series
links: 14.3#partial-sums
An infinite sum $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n = a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + \cdots$. It converges to the sum $S$ if its partial sums $S_N$ converge to $S$, and diverges otherwise.

## partial sum
links: 14.3#partial-sums
$S_N = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_N$, the sum of the first $N$ terms of a series.

## sum (of a series)
links: 14.3#partial-sums
The limit $S = \lim_{N\to\infty} S_N$ of the partial sums of a convergent series, written $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n = S$. Changing finitely many terms does not change whether a series converges.

## geometric series
links: 14.3#geometric
$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} ar^n = a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots$, with first term $a \ne 0$ and ratio $r$: it converges to $\dfrac{a}{1 - r}$ if $\abs{r} < 1$ and diverges if $\abs{r} \ge 1$.

## divergence test
links: 14.4#harmonic
If $\sum a_n$ converges, then $a_n \to 0$. So if $a_n$ does not tend to 0, the series diverges; if $a_n \to 0$, the test says nothing.

## harmonic series
links: 14.4#harmonic-series
$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \dfrac1n = 1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \cdots$. It diverges, though its terms tend to 0: its partial sums grow like $\ln N$.

## integral test
links: 14.5#integral-test
If $f$ is continuous, positive and decreasing on $[1, \infty)$ and $a_n = f(n)$, then $\sum a_n$ and $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx$ both converge or both diverge. The same holds from any starting index $k$.

## $p$-series
sort: p-series
links: 14.5#p-series
$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \dfrac{1}{n^p}$: it converges if $p > 1$ and diverges if $p \le 1$.

## remainder
links: 14.5#remainder 15.5#taylor-theorem
(1) For a series that converges to $S$, $R_N = S - S_N$, the error of the $N$th partial sum; under the hypotheses of the integral test, $\int_{N+1}^\infty f \le R_N \le \int_N^\infty f$. (2) For a Taylor polynomial, $R_n(x) = f(x) - T_n(x)$.

## harmonic number
links: 14.5#slow
$H_N = 1 + \tfrac12 + \dots + \tfrac1N$, the $N$th partial sum of the harmonic series.

## Euler's constant
links: 14.5#slow
$\gamma \approx 0.5772$, the limit of $H_N - \ln N$ as $N \to \infty$.

## alternating series
links: 14.7#alternating
A series whose terms alternate in sign, such as $\sum (-1)^{n-1}b_n = b_1 - b_2 + b_3 - \cdots$ with $b_n > 0$.

## alternating series test
links: 14.7#alternating
If $b_n > 0$, $b_{n+1} \le b_n$ for every $n$ and $b_n \to 0$, then $\sum (-1)^{n-1}b_n$ converges, and its sum $S$ satisfies $\abs{S - S_N} \le b_{N+1}$: the error is at most the first term left out.

## alternating harmonic series
links: 14.7#alternating 15.3#endpoints
$1 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 - \tfrac14 + \cdots$. It converges, to $\ln 2$, but only conditionally.

## absolute convergence
links: 14.8#absolute
$\sum a_n$ converges absolutely if $\sum \abs{a_n}$ converges. Absolute convergence implies convergence, and every rearrangement of an absolutely convergent series has the same sum.

## conditional convergence
links: 14.8#absolute 14.8#rearrangements
$\sum a_n$ converges conditionally if it converges but $\sum \abs{a_n}$ diverges. Rearranging such a series can change its sum.

## rearrangement
links: 14.8#rearrangements
The same terms of a series added in another order.

## Riemann's rearrangement theorem
links: 14.8#rearrangements
If $\sum a_n$ converges conditionally, then for every number $T$ some rearrangement converges to $T$, and others diverge.

## ratio test
links: 14.8#ratio
If $a_n \ne 0$ and $\abs{a_{n+1}/a_n} \to \rho$, a number or $\infty$: for $\rho < 1$, $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely; for $\rho > 1$ or $\rho = \infty$, it diverges; for $\rho = 1$, the test gives no conclusion.

## root test
links: 14.8#root
If $\abs{a_n}^{1/n} \to \rho$, a number or $\infty$: for $\rho < 1$, $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely; for $\rho > 1$ or $\rho = \infty$, it diverges; for $\rho = 1$, no conclusion.

## strategy for testing series
links: 14.9#strategy
First the divergence test; then the series you know (geometric, $p$-series); comparison for terms built from powers of $n$; the ratio test for factorials and exponentials; the root test for $n$th powers; the integral test for $f(n)$ with $f$ continuous, positive and decreasing; for signs that alternate, test $\sum\abs{a_n}$ first.

## power series
links: 15.1#power-series
A series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(x - a)^n$, centred at $a$, with constant coefficients $c_n$. Where it converges, its sum is a function of $x$.

## radius of convergence
links: 15.1#convergence
The number $R$ such that a power series $\sum c_n(x - a)^n$ converges absolutely for $\abs{x - a} < R$ and diverges for $\abs{x - a} > R$; $R = 0$ if it converges only at $a$, and $R = \infty$ if it converges for every $x$.

## interval of convergence
links: 15.1#convergence
The set of all $x$ at which a power series converges: the interval from $a - R$ to $a + R$, with each endpoint included or not, which must be tested separately.

## term-by-term differentiation and integration
links: 15.3#term-by-term
A power series with radius of convergence $R > 0$ can be differentiated and integrated term by term for $\abs{x - a} < R$, and the new series have the same radius $R$.

## Abel's theorem
links: 15.3#endpoints
If a power series with radius $0 < R < \infty$ converges at an endpoint, its sum there is the limit of its sums inside the interval, as $x$ approaches that endpoint.

## Taylor series
links: 15.4#taylor-series
For $f$ with derivatives of every order at $a$, the power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n$. If $f$ is the sum of a power series centred at $a$, that series is its Taylor series.

## Taylor polynomial
links: 15.4#taylor-series
$T_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \dfrac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x - a)^k$, the partial sum of degree $n$ of the Taylor series of $f$ about $a$. $T_1$ is the linearisation.

## Maclaurin series
links: 15.4#taylor-series
A Taylor series about $a = 0$; its partial sums are the Maclaurin polynomials.

## binomial series
links: 15.4#standard
$(1 + x)^k = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \dbinom{k}{n}x^n$, for every real $k$ and every $x$ with $\abs{x} < 1$: the Maclaurin series of $(1 + x)^k$.

## Taylor's theorem
links: 15.5#taylor-theorem
If $f$ has derivatives through order $n+1$ on an open interval containing $a$ and $x\ne a$, there is a $c$ strictly between them with $f(x)-T_n(x)=\tfrac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}$. At the centre the remainder is zero.

## Taylor's inequality
links: 15.5#taylor-theorem
If $\abs{f^{(n+1)}(t)} \le M$ for every $t$ between $a$ and $x$, then $\abs{f(x) - T_n(x)} \le \dfrac{M}{(n + 1)!}\abs{x - a}^{n+1}$.

## Lagrange form (of the remainder)
links: 15.5#taylor-theorem
$R_n(x) = \dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n + 1)!}(x - a)^{n+1}$, for some $c$ between $a$ and $x$: the remainder in Taylor's theorem.

## analytic
links: 15.5#series-equals-function
A function is analytic on an open interval if, around each point of it, it equals the sum of its Taylor series on an interval. $e^x$, $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are analytic on the whole line.

## Machin's formula
links: 15.6#constants
$\dfrac{\pi}{4} = 4\arctan\dfrac15 - \arctan\dfrac{1}{239}$: with the series of $\arctan$ at these small numbers, it computes $\pi$ fast.

## range reduction
links: 15.6#libraries
The first step of a mathematics library computing $\sin x$ or $e^x$: use the function's symmetries to replace $x$ by a small number, for which a polynomial is accurate.

## Horner's rule
links: 15.6#libraries
Evaluating a polynomial from the inside out, $c_0 + u\big(c_1 + u(c_2 + \cdots)\big)$: one multiplication and one addition per coefficient.

## minimax polynomial
links: 15.6#libraries
A polynomial whose coefficients are tuned to spread the error evenly over an interval, so that a lower degree than a Taylor polynomial's reaches full precision. Mathematics libraries use them.

## complex number
links: 15.7#complex
A number $z = p + qi$, where $p$ and $q$ are real and $i^2 = -1$: $p$ is its real part and $q$ its imaginary part. Complex numbers are added and multiplied like expressions in $i$, with $i^2$ replaced by $-1$.

## imaginary unit
links: 15.7#complex
The number $i$ with $i^2 = -1$.

## modulus
links: 15.7#complex
$\abs{z} = \sqrt{p^2 + q^2}$ for $z = p + qi$: the distance from $z$ to 0 in the complex plane.

## complex plane
links: 15.7#complex
The plane in which $z = p + qi$ is the point $(p, q)$, with real parts along the horizontal axis and imaginary parts up the vertical one.

## complex exponential
links: 15.7#complex
$e^z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{z^n}{n!} = 1 + z + \dfrac{z^2}{2!} + \cdots$, for every complex number $z$.

## Euler's formula
links: 15.7#euler-formula 15.7#oscillations
$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ for every real $\theta$. It explains why complex roots of a characteristic equation give solutions with $\cos$ and $\sin$.

## parametric curve
links: 16.1#parametric
The path traced by the point $\big(f(t), g(t)\big)$ as the parameter $t$ runs through an interval; $x = f(t)$, $y = g(t)$ are its parametric equations. On $[a, b]$ it starts at the initial point $\big(f(a), g(a)\big)$ and ends at the terminal point $\big(f(b), g(b)\big)$.

## parameter
links: 16.1#parametric
The variable, usually $t$, whose values give the points $\big(f(t), g(t)\big)$ of a parametric curve.

## parametrisation
links: 16.1#parametric 20.6#parametric-surfaces
Equations $x = f(t)$, $y = g(t)$ (or a vector function $\vb{r}(t)$) that trace a given curve; for a surface, a function $\vb{r}(u, v)$ whose values fill it.

## orientation
links: 16.1#parametric 20.6#flux
The direction in which a parametrised curve is traced as $t$ increases. For a surface, a choice of unit normal at each point, varying continuously, which says which side counts as positive.

## eliminating the parameter
links: 16.1#eliminating
Turning parametric equations into one equation in $x$ and $y$: solve one for $t$ and substitute into the other, or combine them, as $\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$ does.

## ellipse
links: 16.1#parametric
A circle stretched by different factors along two perpendicular directions: $x = 3\cos t$, $y = 2\sin t$ traces the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{9} + \dfrac{y^2}{4} = 1$.

## cycloid
links: 16.1#cycloid
The curve traced by a point on the rim of a wheel of radius $r$ rolling along a line: $x = r(t - \sin t)$, $y = r(1 - \cos t)$. Each arch, for $t$ from 0 to $2\pi$, meets the next in a cusp.

## Lissajous curve
links: 16.1#cycloid
A curve traced when $x$ and $y$ oscillate at different rates, such as $x = \sin 2t$, $y = \sin 3t$. It appears on an oscilloscope driven by two sine waves, and the ratio of their frequencies can be read off it.

## slope of a parametric curve
links: 16.2#tangents
$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$ where $dx/dt \ne 0$, if $f'$ is continuous. The tangent is horizontal where $dy/dt = 0 \ne dx/dt$, and vertical where $dx/dt = 0 \ne dy/dt$.

## double-angle formulas
links: 16.2#tangents
$\sin 2u = 2\sin u\cos u$ and $\cos 2u = \cos^2 u - \sin^2 u = 1 - 2\sin^2 u$: the addition formulas with both angles equal.

## uniformly continuous
links: 16.2#arc-length
$g$ is uniformly continuous on an interval if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a $\delta > 0$ with $\abs{g(s) - g(s')} < \varepsilon$ for all points $s$, $s'$ of the interval with $\abs{s - s'} < \delta$. A function continuous on a closed interval is uniformly continuous there.

## polar coordinates
links: 16.3#polar 19.4#polar
A point has polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ if $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$: for $r > 0$, its distance from the origin (the pole) and the angle from the positive $x$-axis (the polar axis), measured anticlockwise. In polar coordinates $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$.

## pole
links: 16.3#polar
The origin, in polar coordinates; it is $(0, \theta)$ for every $\theta$. The polar axis is the positive $x$-axis.

## cardioid
links: 16.3#polar-curves
The heart-shaped polar curve $r = 1 + \cos\theta$.

## rose
links: 16.3#polar-curves
A flower-shaped polar curve such as $r = \cos 3\theta$, which has three petals.

## Archimedean spiral
links: 16.3#polar-curves
A spiral such as $r = \theta/2$, $\theta \ge 0$: each full turn adds the same amount to $r$, so successive turns are the same distance apart.

## area in polar coordinates
links: 16.3#polar-area
For $f$ continuous and $f \ge 0$ on $[\alpha, \beta]$, $0 < \beta - \alpha \le 2\pi$, the region bounded by $r = f(\theta)$ and the rays $\theta = \alpha$ and $\theta = \beta$ has area $\int_\alpha^\beta \tfrac12 f(\theta)^2\,d\theta$.

## elliptic integral
links: 16.3#polar-area
An integral such as the perimeter of an ellipse, whose integrand has no elementary antiderivative, so that it is computed numerically.

## right-hand rule
links: 16.4#space 16.6#cross 20.7#stokes
When the fingers of your right hand curl from the first direction towards the second, your thumb points along the third: so the $x$-, $y$- and $z$-axes are arranged, $\vb{a}\times\vb{b}$ points, and the normal of a surface goes with its boundary in Stokes' theorem.

## sphere
links: 16.4#space
The points at distance $R$ from a point $(a, b, c)$: $(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 + (z - c)^2 = R^2$.

## vector
links: 16.4#components
A quantity with a length and a direction, drawn as an arrow; arrows with the same length and direction are the same vector. In components, $\vb{a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3\rangle$ in space and $\langle a_1, a_2\rangle$ in the plane.

## component
links: 16.4#components 20.1#fields
One of the numbers $a_1, a_2, a_3$ of a vector $\langle a_1, a_2, a_3\rangle$: the vector from $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ to $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ has components $x_2 - x_1$, $y_2 - y_1$, $z_2 - z_1$. A vector field has component functions $P$, $Q$, $R$.

## length (of a vector)
links: 16.4#components
$\norm{\vb{a}} = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}$, also called its magnitude or norm.

## position vector
links: 16.4#components
The vector from the origin to a point $(a_1, a_2, a_3)$, which is $\langle a_1, a_2, a_3\rangle$.

## zero vector
links: 16.4#components
$\vb{0} = \langle 0, 0, 0\rangle$, which has length 0 and no direction.

## scalar
links: 16.4#components
An ordinary number, as opposed to a vector.

## scalar multiple
links: 16.4#arithmetic
$c\vb{a} = \langle ca_1, ca_2, ca_3\rangle$: $\vb{a}$ stretched by the factor $\abs{c}$, and reversed if $c < 0$.

## vector sum
links: 16.4#arithmetic
$\vb{a} + \vb{b} = \langle a_1 + b_1, a_2 + b_2, a_3 + b_3\rangle$: walk along $\vb{a}$ and then along $\vb{b}$. The difference is $\vb{a} - \vb{b} = \vb{a} + (-1)\vb{b}$.

## parallel
links: 16.4#arithmetic
Two nonzero vectors are parallel if one is a scalar multiple of the other.

## unit vector
links: 16.4#arithmetic
A vector of length 1. $\vb{a}/\norm{\vb{a}}$ is the unit vector in the direction of $\vb{a} \ne \vb{0}$; finding it is normalising $\vb{a}$.

## standard unit vectors
links: 16.4#arithmetic
$\vb{i} = \langle 1, 0, 0\rangle$, $\vb{j} = \langle 0, 1, 0\rangle$ and $\vb{k} = \langle 0, 0, 1\rangle$, so that $\langle a_1, a_2, a_3\rangle = a_1\vb{i} + a_2\vb{j} + a_3\vb{k}$.

## force
links: 16.4#arithmetic
A vector with the direction of a push or pull and its strength, in newtons, as its length.

## dot product
links: 16.5#dot
$\vb{a}\cdot\vb{b} = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3$, a number, also called the scalar product. For nonzero vectors at angle $\theta$, $0 \le \theta \le \pi$, $\vb{a}\cdot\vb{b} = \norm{\vb{a}}\,\norm{\vb{b}}\cos\theta$.

## law of cosines
links: 16.5#dot
In a triangle with sides $p$, $q$ and $s$, where $\gamma$ is the angle between $p$ and $q$: $s^2 = p^2 + q^2 - 2pq\cos\gamma$.

## orthogonal
links: 16.5#dot
Perpendicular: $\vb{a}\cdot\vb{b} = 0$. The zero vector counts as orthogonal to every vector.

## cosine similarity
links: 16.5#angles
The cosine of the angle between two vectors, $\dfrac{\vb{a}\cdot\vb{b}}{\norm{\vb{a}}\,\norm{\vb{b}}}$, which search engines and language models use to measure how alike two documents or words are.

## Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
sort: Cauchy Schwarz inequality
links: ex16.11
$\abs{\vb{a}\cdot\vb{b}} \le \norm{\vb{a}}\,\norm{\vb{b}}$ for all vectors $\vb{a}$ and $\vb{b}$.

## scalar projection
links: 16.5#projections
$\operatorname{comp}_{\vb{a}}\vb{b} = \dfrac{\vb{a}\cdot\vb{b}}{\norm{\vb{a}}}$, for $\vb{a} \ne \vb{0}$: the signed length $\norm{\vb{b}}\cos\theta$ of the shadow of $\vb{b}$ on the line of $\vb{a}$.

## vector projection
links: 16.5#projections
$\operatorname{proj}_{\vb{a}}\vb{b} = \dfrac{\vb{a}\cdot\vb{b}}{\vb{a}\cdot\vb{a}}\,\vb{a}$, for $\vb{a} \ne \vb{0}$: the shadow of $\vb{b}$ on the line of $\vb{a}$, the part of $\vb{b}$ that points along $\vb{a}$.

## cross product
links: 16.6#cross
$\vb{a}\times\vb{b} = \langle a_2b_3 - a_3b_2,\ a_3b_1 - a_1b_3,\ a_1b_2 - a_2b_1\rangle$, for vectors in space. It is orthogonal to $\vb{a}$ and $\vb{b}$, has length $\norm{\vb{a}}\,\norm{\vb{b}}\sin\theta$ (the area of their parallelogram), and $\vb{b}\times\vb{a} = -\vb{a}\times\vb{b}$.

## determinant
links: 16.6#cross 18.2#second-derivative-test 19.8#change-of-variables
The number $ps - qr$ of the $2 \times 2$ array with rows $p, q$ and $r, s$. Each component of a cross product is one; so are the determinant $D$ of the Hessian and the Jacobian.

## Lagrange's identity
links: 16.6#cross
$\norm{\vb{a}\times\vb{b}}^2 = \norm{\vb{a}}^2\norm{\vb{b}}^2 - (\vb{a}\cdot\vb{b})^2$.

## torque
links: 16.6#cross
The turning effect $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \vb{r}\times\vb{F}$ of a force $\vb{F}$ applied at the point with position vector $\vb{r}$ from the axis.

## vector equation of a line
links: 16.7#lines
$\vb{r} = \vb{r}_0 + t\vb{v}$: the line through the point with position vector $\vb{r}_0$ in the direction of $\vb{v} \ne \vb{0}$. In components it gives parametric equations; solving each for $t$ gives symmetric equations.

## symmetric equations
links: 16.7#lines
$\dfrac{x - x_0}{a} = \dfrac{y - y_0}{b} = \dfrac{z - z_0}{c}$: the line through $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ in the direction $\langle a, b, c\rangle$, when $a$, $b$ and $c$ are all nonzero.

## skew lines
links: 16.7#lines
Lines in space that are not parallel and never meet.

## normal vector
links: 16.7#planes 20.6#parametric-surfaces
A nonzero vector perpendicular to a plane or surface. The plane through $P_0$ with normal $\vb{n} = \langle a, b, c\rangle$ is $a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) + c(z - z_0) = 0$; a parametric surface has the normal $\vb{r}_u\times\vb{r}_v$.

## distance from a point to a plane
links: 16.7#planes
The distance from $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ to the plane $ax + by + cz = d$ is $\dfrac{\abs{ax_1 + by_1 + cz_1 - d}}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}$.

## vector function
links: 16.8#motion
A function $\vb{r}(t) = \langle f(t), g(t), h(t)\rangle$ that assigns a vector to each number $t$ of its domain; $f$, $g$ and $h$ are its component functions. Limits, continuity and derivatives are taken component by component.

## space curve
links: 16.8#motion
The curve traced by the tip of $\vb{r}(t)$, drawn from the origin, as $t$ varies.

## helix
links: 16.8#motion
The space curve $\vb{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, t\rangle$, which winds round the cylinder $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, climbing $2\pi$ in each turn: the shape of a spring.

## derivative of a vector function
links: 16.8#velocity
$\vb{r}'(t) = \lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{\vb{r}(t + h) - \vb{r}(t)}{h} = \langle f'(t), g'(t), h'(t)\rangle$. Where it is not $\vb{0}$, it is a tangent vector, pointing the way the curve is traced.

## unit tangent vector
links: 16.8#integrals
$\vb{T}(t) = \dfrac{\vb{r}'(t)}{\norm{\vb{r}'(t)}}$, where $\vb{r}'(t) \ne \vb{0}$.

## arc length function
links: 16.8#integrals
$s(t) = \int_a^t \norm{\vb{r}'(u)}\,du$, the distance travelled along a curve since time $a$; $ds/dt = \norm{\vb{r}'(t)}$.

## projectile motion
links: 16.8#projectiles
Motion under gravity alone, $\vb{a} = \langle 0, -g\rangle$. Launched at speed $v_0$ and angle $\alpha$ on level ground, a projectile lands after the flight time $\dfrac{2v_0\sin\alpha}{g}$, having travelled the range $\dfrac{v_0^2\sin 2\alpha}{g}$.

## curvature
links: 16.8#projectiles
$\kappa = \Big\lVert\dfrac{d\vb{T}}{ds}\Big\rVert = \dfrac{\norm{\vb{T}'(t)}}{\norm{\vb{r}'(t)}}$: how fast the direction of a curve turns per unit of distance travelled. A circle of radius $R$ has curvature $1/R$.

## centripetal acceleration
links: ex16.10
The acceleration of uniform motion round a circle of radius $R$ at speed $v$: it points to the centre and has length $v^2/R$.

## astroid
links: ex6.11 ex16.6
The curve $x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = 1$, traced by $x = \cos^3 t$, $y = \sin^3 t$.

## inverse hyperbolic sine
links: ex6.10
The inverse of $\sinh$, which is strictly increasing on $\R$: $\operatorname{arsinh} x = \ln\big(x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1}\big)$.

## tetrahedral angle
links: 16.5#angles
The angle between two lines from the centre of a regular tetrahedron to its vertices, $\arccos(-1/3)$.
