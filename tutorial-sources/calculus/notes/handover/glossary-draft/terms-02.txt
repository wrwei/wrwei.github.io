#! Modules 4 to 8

## velocity
links: 4.1#tangents 4.6#motion 16.8#velocity
For an object moving along a line with position $s(t)$, the instantaneous velocity at time $a$ is $v(a) = \lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{s(a + h) - s(a)}{h} = s'(a)$, when the limit exists. In space it is the vector $\vb{v}(t) = \vb{r}'(t)$.

## speed
links: 4.1#tangents 16.8#velocity
The size of the velocity: $\abs{v(a)}$ on a line, $\norm{\vb{v}(t)}$ in space.

## instantaneous rate of change
links: 4.2#derivative
The derivative $f'(a)$, measured in units of $f$ per unit of $x$: the limit of average rates of change over shorter and shorter intervals.

## differentiable
links: 4.2#derivative 4.4#failures
$f$ is differentiable at $a$ if $f'(a)$ exists as a real number. It is differentiable on an open interval if it is differentiable at every point of it, and on $[a, b]$ if it is differentiable on $(a, b)$ and the right-hand derivative at $a$ and the left-hand derivative at $b$ exist.

## derivative function
links: 4.3#derivative-function
The function $f'$ with $f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$ at every $x$ where this limit exists. Its domain may be smaller than that of $f$.

## increasing
links: 4.3#graph-of-derivative 8.3#monotonic 14.2#monotone
(1) A function $f$ is increasing on an interval if $x_1 < x_2$ in it always gives $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$; some books call this strictly increasing. (2) A sequence $(a_n)$ is increasing if $a_n \le a_{n+1}$ for every $n$, so equal neighbours are allowed.

## decreasing
links: 4.3#graph-of-derivative 8.3#monotonic 14.2#monotone
(1) A function $f$ is decreasing on an interval if $x_1 < x_2$ in it always gives $f(x_1) > f(x_2)$. (2) A sequence $(a_n)$ is decreasing if $a_n \ge a_{n+1}$ for every $n$.

## Leibniz's notation
links: 4.3#notation
Writing the derivative of $y = f(x)$ as $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, which shows which variable changes and recalls the quotient $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ whose limit it is. It is not a fraction, but often behaves like one.

## right-hand derivative
links: 4.4#failures
$f'_+(a) = \lim_{h\to0^+}\dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$, when this limit exists; the left-hand derivative $f'_-(a)$ takes $h \to 0^-$. $f'(a)$ exists exactly when both exist and are equal.

## corner
links: 4.4#failures
A point where both one-sided derivatives exist but differ, as for $\abs{x}$ at 0: no single line is the tangent there.

## cusp
links: 4.4#failures 16.2#tangents
A sharp point of a graph where the difference quotients of a continuous function tend to $\infty$ on one side and $-\infty$ on the other, as for $x^{2/3}$ at 0; also where two arches of a cycloid meet.

## vertical tangent
links: 4.4#failures
The line $x = a$, when $f$ is continuous at $a$ and its difference quotients tend to $\infty$ from both sides, or to $-\infty$ from both sides, as for $x^{1/3}$ at 0. $f'(a)$ does not exist.

## differentiable implies continuous
links: 4.4#continuity 17.4#differentiability
If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$. The converse is false: $\abs{x}$ is continuous but not differentiable at 0. The same holds in two variables.

## power rule
links: 4.5#power-rule 5.3#negative-powers 6.6#real-powers
$\dfrac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}$: for every $x$ when $n$ is a positive integer (Module 4), for $x \ne 0$ when $n$ is an integer $\le 0$ (Module 5), and for $x > 0$ when $n$ is any real number (Module 6).

## constant rule
links: 4.5#power-rule
The derivative of a constant function is 0: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(c) = 0$.

## binomial theorem
links: 4.5#power-rule
$(x + h)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\dbinom{n}{k}x^{n-k}h^k$ for every positive integer $n$: the term $x^{n-k}h^k$ arises once for each way of choosing which $k$ of the $n$ factors supply an $h$.

## binomial coefficient
links: 4.5#power-rule 15.4#standard
$\dbinom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!\,(n - k)!}$, read "n choose k": the number of ways to choose $k$ things from $n$. For any real $k$, $\dbinom{k}{n} = \dfrac{k(k - 1)\cdots(k - n + 1)}{n!}$, the coefficients of the binomial series.

## constant multiple rule
links: 4.5#sum-rule
If $f$ is differentiable at $x$ and $c$ is a constant, then $(cf)'(x) = c\,f'(x)$.

## sum rule
links: 4.5#sum-rule
If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at $x$, then $(f + g)'(x) = f'(x) + g'(x)$, and $(f - g)'(x) = f'(x) - g'(x)$.

## linearity
links: 4.5#sum-rule 9.5#properties
The constant multiple and sum rules together: differentiation is linear, $(cf + g)' = cf' + g'$. Integration is linear too: $\int_a^b \big(cf(x) + g(x)\big)\,dx = c\int_a^b f(x)\,dx + \int_a^b g(x)\,dx$.

## higher derivatives
links: 4.6#higher-derivatives
The second derivative $f'' = (f')'$, the third derivative $f''' = (f'')'$, and in general the $n$th derivative $f^{(n)}$, the derivative of $f^{(n-1)}$, wherever these exist. In Leibniz's notation, $f''(x) = \dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$.

## acceleration
links: 4.6#motion 16.8#velocity
The rate of change of velocity: $a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t)$ on a line, $\vb{a}(t) = \vb{v}'(t) = \vb{r}''(t)$ in space. An object speeds up when its velocity and acceleration have the same sign, and slows down when their signs differ.

## jerk
links: 4.6#motion
The rate of change of acceleration, $j(t) = a'(t) = s'''(t)$: what you feel as a jolt when a lift starts or stops abruptly.

## forward difference
links: 4.7#differences
$\dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$, for a step $h > 0$: an estimate of $f'(a)$ with error $O(h)$. The backward difference $\dfrac{f(a) - f(a - h)}{h}$ uses $-h$ in place of $h$.

## central difference
links: 4.7#differences
$\dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a - h)}{2h}$, for a step $h > 0$: the average of the forward and backward differences, an estimate of $f'(a)$ with error $O(h^2)$.

## error (of an approximation)
links: 4.7#differences 7.3#errors 9.3#riemann-sums
The exact value minus the approximation. It is positive when the approximation is too small and negative when it is too large.

## truncation error
links: 4.7#truncation
The error of a method that remains when the arithmetic is exact, because it stops at a step $h > 0$ instead of going to the limit.

## order (of a method)
links: 4.7#truncation 13.4#euler-error
A method whose error is $O(h^p)$, and no better, has order $p$. The forward difference and Euler's method have order 1, the central difference order 2, RK4 order 4.

## machine epsilon
links: 4.7#rounding
The gap between 1 and the next larger floating-point number: $2^{-52} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$.

## rounding error
links: 4.7#rounding
The part of an error that comes from the computer's rounding. In a difference quotient its size is about $\varepsilon\abs{f(a)}/h$, where $\varepsilon$ is the machine epsilon, so it grows as $h$ shrinks.

## product rule
links: 5.2#rectangle
If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at $x$, then so is $fg$, and $(fg)'(x) = f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x)$.

## quotient rule
links: 5.3#quotient
If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at $x$ and $g(x) \ne 0$, then $\Big(\dfrac{f}{g}\Big)'(x) = \dfrac{f'(x)\,g(x) - f(x)\,g'(x)}{g(x)^2}$.

## chain rule
links: 5.5#rates-multiply 17.5#chain-rule
If $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ at $g(x)$, then $\big(f(g(x))\big)' = f'\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)$; in Leibniz's notation, $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du}\,\dfrac{du}{dx}$: rates multiply. In several variables, $\dfrac{dz}{dt} = \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\dfrac{dx}{dt} + \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\dfrac{dy}{dt}$.

## finite differences
links: 5.8#autodiff
Estimating a derivative by running a program at $a$ and at $a + h$ and computing the difference quotient. It needs nothing but the program, but a large $h$ gives a truncation error and a small one a rounding error.

## symbolic differentiation
links: 5.8#autodiff
Differentiating a formula exactly by the rules of differentiation, as SymPy does.

## automatic differentiation
links: 5.8#autodiff 5.8#backprop
Computing the derivative of a program by running it once on numbers that carry a derivative along with their value, updated at every step by the rules of differentiation: exact apart from rounding. Dual numbers do it in forward mode.

## dual number
links: 5.8#autodiff
An expression $a + b\varepsilon$ with real $a$ and $b$ and a new symbol $\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon^2 = 0$; $b$ is its $\varepsilon$-part. For a polynomial $p$, $p(a + \varepsilon) = p(a) + p'(a)\,\varepsilon$, so the $\varepsilon$-part carries the derivative.

## forward mode
links: 5.8#backprop
Automatic differentiation that carries derivatives forwards, from the input through each step of the program to the output, as dual numbers do.

## backpropagation
links: 5.8#backprop 17.8#backpropagation
Automatic differentiation in the backward direction: run the computation once and keep the values (the forward pass), then go back from the output to the inputs, multiplying local derivatives by the chain rule (the backward pass). It gives the derivative of a loss with respect to every parameter at a cost of a small multiple of computing the loss.

## continuous compounding
links: 6.1#compound-interest
Adding interest continually, at every instant: the limit as $n \to \infty$ of adding it $n$ times a year. At 100% a year, £1 becomes $\lim_{n\to\infty}(1 + 1/n)^n = e$ pounds.

## convex
links: 6.1#base-e 8.4#concavity
A function whose graph is concave up, bending upwards, is called convex in optimisation and machine learning; one whose graph is concave down is called concave.

## derivative of an inverse function
links: 6.2#inverse-functions
If $f$ is continuous and one-to-one on an open interval and differentiable at $a$ with $f'(a) \ne 0$, then $(f^{-1})'(b) = \dfrac{1}{f'(a)}$ at $b = f(a)$.

## implicit differentiation
links: 6.4#implicit
Differentiating both sides of an equation in $x$ and $y$, such as $x^2 + y^2 = 25$, with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a function of $x$ by the chain rule, then solving for $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$.

## inverse trigonometric functions
links: 6.5#inverse-trig
$\arcsin x$ is the $y$ in $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ with $\sin y = x$, for $-1 \le x \le 1$; $\arccos x$ the $y$ in $[0, \pi]$ with $\cos y = x$, for $-1 \le x \le 1$; and $\arctan x$ the $y$ in $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ with $\tan y = x$, for every real $x$.

## arcsec
links: 6.5#inverse-trig
For $\abs{x} \ge 1$, this series takes $\arcsec x$ to be the $y$ in $[0, \pi/2) \cup (\pi/2, \pi]$ with $\sec y = x$, that is, $\arccos(1/x)$. Books differ about its range, and so about the sign of its derivative for $x < -1$.

## logarithmic differentiation
links: 6.6#real-powers
Differentiating a function $y$ built from products, quotients and powers by taking $\ln\abs{y}$, expanding with the laws of logarithms, differentiating ($y'/y$ on the left) and multiplying by $y$. It works where $y \ne 0$.

## hyperbolic functions
links: 6.7#hyperbolic
$\sinh x = \dfrac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2}$, $\cosh x = \dfrac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2}$ and $\tanh x = \dfrac{\sinh x}{\cosh x}$, for every real $x$: the hyperbolic sine, cosine and tangent. $\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1$.

## hyperbola
links: 6.7#hyperbolic
The curve $x^2 - y^2 = 1$; the point $(\cosh t, \sinh t)$ moves along its right-hand branch, as $(\cos t, \sin t)$ moves round the unit circle.

## catenary
links: 6.7#hanging-chain
The shape of a chain or cable hanging under its own weight: the graph of $y = a\cosh(x/a)$, with its lowest point at $(0, a)$.

## differential equation
links: 6.8#growth-decay 10.2#initial-value 13.1#what
An equation that involves an unknown function and one or more of its derivatives, such as $y' = ky$. Its order is the order of the highest derivative in it.

## exponential growth and decay
links: 6.8#growth-decay 13.5#growth-decay
The solutions of $y' = ky$, for a constant $k$: exactly the functions $y = Ce^{kt}$, with $C = y(0)$. For $k > 0$ they grow, for $k < 0$ they decay. Module 6 checks that they are solutions; Module 13 proves that there are no others.

## doubling time
links: 6.8#doubling
The time $T = \dfrac{\ln 2}{k}$ in which $y = Ce^{kt}$, with $k > 0$, doubles, whatever the starting value and whenever the clock starts.

## half-life
links: 6.8#doubling
The time $T_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\abs{k}}$ in which $y = Ce^{kt}$, with $k < 0$, halves.

## related rates
links: 7.1#related-rates
Problems in which two quantities linked by an equation both change with time: differentiating the equation with respect to time, by the chain rule, links their rates of change.

## linearisation
links: 7.2#linear-approximation 17.4#tangent-planes
The linearisation of $f$ at $a$ is $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$, whose graph is the tangent line at $(a, f(a))$; $f$ must be differentiable at $a$. In two variables, $L(x, y) = f(a, b) + f_x(a, b)(x - a) + f_y(a, b)(y - b)$.

## linear approximation
links: 7.2#linear-approximation 17.4#tangent-planes
Using $f(x) \approx L(x)$, the linearisation at $a$, for $x$ near $a$; also called the tangent line approximation. Its error is small even compared with $x - a$.

## differential
links: 7.2#differentials 17.4#differential
For $y = f(x)$ with $f$ differentiable, the differential $dx$ is an independent variable, any real number, and the differential $dy = f'(x)\,dx$ is the change along the tangent line. $\Delta y \approx dy$ when $dx$ is small.

## absolute error
links: 7.3#errors
The absolute value $\abs{\Delta x}$ of the error $\Delta x = x - \tilde x$ of an approximation $\tilde x$ of $x$.

## relative error
links: 7.3#errors
$\abs{\Delta x}/\abs{x}$, for $x \ne 0$: the size of the error compared with the size of the true value. Written as a percentage, it is the percentage error.

## condition number
links: 7.3#errors 18.6#ill-conditioning
(1) $\abs{xf'(x)/f(x)}$, the factor by which computing $f(x)$ multiplies the relative error of $x$. (2) For the bowl $\tfrac12(ax^2 + cy^2)$, $0 < a \le c$, the ratio $\kappa = c/a$; gradient descent is slow when it is large.

## Newton's method
links: 7.4#newton
To solve $f(x) = 0$ for differentiable $f$: choose $x_0$ and compute $x_{n+1} = x_n - \dfrac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$ while $f'(x_n) \ne 0$. Each step follows the tangent line to the $x$-axis. Also called the Newton–Raphson method.

## iterate
links: 7.4#newton
One of the numbers $x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots$ that a repeated method, such as Newton's method, produces.

## correct to $d$ decimal places
sort: correct to d decimal places
links: 7.4#newton
An approximation $\tilde x$ of $r$ is correct to $d$ decimal places if $\abs{r - \tilde x} < 0.5 \times 10^{-d}$.

## quadratic convergence
links: 7.4#quadratic
Convergence in which each error is roughly a fixed multiple of the square of the one before, so that the number of correct digits roughly doubles at each step. Newton's method converges quadratically to a root $r$ with $f'(r) \ne 0$ when $f'$ and $f''$ are continuous near $r$ and $x_0$ is close enough to $r$.

## L'Hôpital's rule
sort: LHopitals rule
links: 7.5#the-rule 8.2#consequences
If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable near $a$, with $g'(x) \ne 0$ there, and $f/g$ has the form $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$ at $a$, then $\lim\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim\dfrac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$ whenever the second limit exists or is $\pm\infty$. It holds for one-sided limits and as $x \to \pm\infty$; Module 8 proves it.

## softplus
links: 7.5#the-rule
The function $s(x) = \ln(1 + e^x)$ of machine learning, a smooth version of $\operatorname{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$.

## absolute maximum
links: 8.1#extrema 18.1#critical-points
$f(c)$ is the absolute (or global) maximum value of $f$ if $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for every $x$ in the domain; the absolute minimum value has $f(c) \le f(x)$. On an interval $I$, the domain is cut down to $I$.

## local maximum
links: 8.1#extrema 18.1#critical-points
$f(c)$ is a local maximum value of $f$ if $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for every $x$ in some open interval that contains $c$ and lies in the domain; a local minimum value has $f(c) \le f(x)$. In this series an endpoint of the domain is never a local extremum. In two variables, an open disc replaces the interval.

## extremum
links: 8.1#extrema 18.1#critical-points
A maximum or minimum value, absolute or local; plural extrema. The same as an extreme value.

## Fermat's theorem
links: 8.1#extrema 18.1#critical-points
If $f$ has a local maximum or minimum at $c$ and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c) = 0$. In two variables: if the first partial derivatives exist at a local extremum $(a, b)$, then $\nabla f(a, b) = \vb{0}$.

## critical number
links: 8.1#extrema
A number $c$ in the domain of $f$ at which $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist. Every local extremum is at a critical number. Some books say critical point.

## closed interval method
links: 8.1#closed-interval
To find the absolute extreme values of a function continuous on $[a, b]$, compare its values at the endpoints and at the critical numbers in $(a, b)$: the largest is the maximum and the smallest the minimum.

## Rolle's theorem
links: 8.2#mvt
If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, differentiable on $(a, b)$, and $f(a) = f(b)$, then $f'(c) = 0$ for some $c$ in $(a, b)$.

## Mean Value Theorem
links: 8.2#mvt
If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, then $f'(c) = \dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$ for some $c$ in $(a, b)$: somewhere the instantaneous rate of change equals the average one.

## Cauchy's mean value theorem
links: 8.2#consequences
If $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, then $\big(f(b) - f(a)\big)g'(c) = \big(g(b) - g(a)\big)f'(c)$ for some $c$ in $(a, b)$. It proves L'Hôpital's rule.

## increasing/decreasing test
links: 8.3#monotonic
Let $f$ be continuous on an interval $I$ and differentiable at its points that are not endpoints. If $f' > 0$ at all of them, $f$ is increasing on $I$; if $f' < 0$, decreasing.

## strictly increasing
links: ex1.10 8.3#monotonic 14.2#monotone
For functions, what this series calls increasing: $x_1 < x_2$ gives $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$. For sequences, $a_n < a_{n+1}$ for every $n$. Strictly decreasing is defined in the same way.

## sign chart
links: 8.3#monotonic
A table of the signs of $f'$ (or $f''$) on the intervals between its zeros and the points where it is undefined, read from the signs of its factors.

## first derivative test
links: 8.3#first-derivative-test 8.6#optimisation
At a critical number $c$ where $f$ is continuous: if $f'$ changes from positive to negative, $f(c)$ is a local maximum; from negative to positive, a local minimum; no change of sign, no extremum. If the signs hold on the whole of an interval $I$ around $c$, $f(c)$ is the absolute maximum or minimum on $I$.

## sigmoid
links: 8.4#concavity 13.6#logistic
The function $\sigma(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$ of machine learning, which turns any real number into a number between 0 and 1. It is the logistic curve with $r = 1$, $K = 1$ and $y(0) = \tfrac12$.

## concave up
links: 8.4#concavity
The graph of $f$ is concave up on an open interval $I$, where $f$ is differentiable, if $f'$ is increasing on $I$; concave down if $f'$ is decreasing. A graph that is concave up lies above its tangent lines.

## concavity test
links: 8.4#concavity
If $f''(x) > 0$ for every $x$ in an open interval $I$, the graph of $f$ is concave up on $I$; if $f''(x) < 0$, concave down.

## inflection point
links: 8.4#concavity
A point $(c, f(c))$ of the graph where $f$ is continuous and the graph changes concavity, from up to down or from down to up. If $f''(c)$ exists there, it is 0, but $f''(c) = 0$ is not enough.

## second derivative test
links: 8.4#second-derivative-test 18.2#second-derivative-test
(1) If $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) > 0$, $f(c)$ is a local minimum; if $f''(c) < 0$, a local maximum; if $f''(c) = 0$, no information. (2) For $f(x, y)$ with continuous second partial derivatives near a point where $\nabla f = \vb{0}$, with $D$ the determinant of the Hessian: $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} > 0$ give a local minimum, $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} < 0$ a local maximum, $D < 0$ a saddle point, $D = 0$ no information.

## curve sketching
links: 8.5#sketching
Drawing the graph of $f$ from its domain, intercepts, symmetry, asymptotes, a sign chart of $f'$ (rise, fall, local extrema) and one of $f''$ (concavity, inflection points).

## optimisation
links: 8.6#optimisation 18.4#lagrange
Finding the largest or smallest value of a quantity: write it as a function of one variable on an interval, find the critical numbers, and show by a theorem that you have the absolute extremum. Module 18 does it in several variables, with or without constraints.

## Fermat's principle
links: 8.6#optimisation
Light takes the path of least time. It implies Snell's law of refraction.

## Snell's law
links: 8.6#optimisation
The law of refraction, $\dfrac{\sin\theta_1}{v_1} = \dfrac{\sin\theta_2}{v_2}$, for light passing between two media in which it has speeds $v_1$ and $v_2$; the angles are measured from the perpendicular to the boundary.

## marginal cost
links: 8.6#optimisation
The derivative $C'(q)$ of a cost function, about the cost $C(q + 1) - C(q)$ of one more unit. The average cost is $C(q)/q$, the cost per unit.

## step size
links: 8.7#gradient-descent 13.4#euler 17.7#gradient-descent
The size of each step of a numerical method: $\eta > 0$ in gradient descent, where machine learning calls it the learning rate; $h > 0$ in Euler's method.

## learning rate
links: 8.7#gradient-descent 18.6#gradient-descent
Machine learning's name for the step size $\eta$ of gradient descent. On the bowl $\tfrac12(ax^2 + cy^2)$ with $0 < a \le c$, gradient descent converges exactly when $\eta < 2/c$.

## loss
links: 8.7#gradient-descent 17.7#gradient-descent
In machine learning, a measure of a model's error on its training data, as a function of the model's parameters; training minimises it by gradient descent.

## contrapositive
links: 4.4#continuity
For an implication from $P$ to $Q$, the equivalent implication from failure of $Q$ to failure of $P$. Since differentiability implies continuity, discontinuity rules out differentiability.

## change of base
links: 6.1#base-e
For a positive base $a$, $a^x=e^{x\ln a}$. For logarithms, $\log_a x=\ln x/\ln a$ when $a\ne1$ and $x>0$.

## hyperbolic sine
links: 6.7#hyperbolic
$\sinh x=(e^x-e^{-x})/2$ for any real $x$.

## hyperbolic cosine
links: 6.7#hyperbolic
$\cosh x=(e^x+e^{-x})/2$ for any real $x$.

## hyperbolic tangent
links: 6.7#hyperbolic
$\tanh x=\sinh x/\cosh x$ for any real $x$.
