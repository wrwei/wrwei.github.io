#! Modules 9 to 13

## definite integral
links: 9.1#area-distance 9.4#definite-integral
For $a < b$, $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the number $I$ that the Riemann sums of $f$ on $[a, b]$ approach as the norm of the partition tends to 0, whatever the sample points, when such a number exists. For $f \ge 0$ it is the area under the graph; in general, the net area.

## sigma notation
links: 9.2#sigma
$\sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i = a_m + a_{m+1} + \dots + a_n$, for whole numbers $m \le n$. The letter $i$ is the index of summation, and $m$ and $n$ are the lower and upper limits of the sum.

## index of summation
links: 9.2#sigma 14.1#sequences
The letter $i$ in $\sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i$. It is a dummy variable: $\sum_{i=1}^{5} i^2$ and $\sum_{k=1}^{5} k^2$ are the same number. The index of a sequence is the $n$ of its term $a_n$.

## dummy variable
links: 9.2#sigma 10.3#area-function
A variable whose name does not matter, such as the index of a sum or the variable of integration: $\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ and $\int_a^x f(s)\,ds$ are the same number.

## rules for sums
links: 9.2#sigma
$\sum c\,a_i = c\sum a_i$, $\sum (a_i + b_i) = \sum a_i + \sum b_i$ (and the same with $-$), and $\sum_{i=1}^{n} c = nc$.

## sums of powers
links: 9.2#power-sums
For every whole number $n \ge 1$: $\sum_{i=1}^{n} i = \dfrac{n(n + 1)}{2}$, $\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^2 = \dfrac{n(n + 1)(2n + 1)}{6}$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^3 = \Big(\dfrac{n(n + 1)}{2}\Big)^2$.

## telescoping
links: 9.2#power-sums 14.3#telescoping
A sum of differences, $(b_1 - b_2) + (b_2 - b_3) + \dots + (b_N - b_{N+1})$, in which everything cancels except $b_1 - b_{N+1}$. A telescoping series converges exactly when $b_n$ has a limit, and then its sum is $b_1 - \lim b_n$.

## mathematical induction
links: ex5.10 9.2#power-sums 14.2#recursive
A way to prove a statement $P(n)$ for every whole number $n \ge 1$: prove the base case $P(1)$, and the inductive step, that $P(k)$ implies $P(k + 1)$ for every $k \ge 1$. Then $P(1)$, $P(2)$, $P(3)$, … follow like falling dominoes.

## base case
links: ex5.10 9.2#power-sums
The first step of a proof by induction: the statement for $n = 1$.

## inductive step
links: ex5.10 9.2#power-sums
The second step of a proof by induction: whenever the statement holds for $k$, it also holds for $k + 1$.

## left sum
links: 9.3#riemann-sums
With $n$ equal subintervals of width $\Delta x = (b - a)/n$ and cut points $x_i = a + i\,\Delta x$: the left sum is $L_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_{i-1})\,\Delta x$, the right sum $R_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x$.

## right sum
links: 9.3#riemann-sums
$R_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x$, with $n$ equal subintervals: each rectangle takes its height at the right end of its subinterval.

## midpoint rule
links: 9.3#riemann-sums 11.7#numerical
The midpoint sum $M_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(\bar x_i)\,\Delta x$, with $n$ equal subintervals and $\bar x_i$ the midpoint of the $i$th, used to approximate an integral. If $f''$ is continuous and $\abs{f''} \le K$ on $[a, b]$, its error is at most $\dfrac{K(b - a)^3}{24n^2}$.

## Riemann sum
links: 9.3#riemann-sums 9.4#definite-integral 19.1#double-integrals
$\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x_i$, for a partition of $[a, b]$ with widths $\Delta x_i$ and a sample point $x_i^*$ in each subinterval: a total of rectangles. The left, right and midpoint sums are Riemann sums; double and triple Riemann sums add up boxes.

## partition
links: 9.4#definite-integral
For $a < b$, a list of points $a = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_n = b$, which cuts $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$ of widths $\Delta x_i = x_i - x_{i-1}$.

## norm (of a partition)
links: 9.4#definite-integral
The largest width of the subintervals of a partition $P$, written $\norm{P}$.

## sample point
links: 9.4#definite-integral 19.1#double-integrals
A point $x_i^*$ chosen in the $i$th subinterval of a partition (or in a small rectangle or box), at which the Riemann sum takes the value of $f$.

## integrable
links: 9.4#definite-integral 19.1#double-integrals
$f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$ if its definite integral there exists. Every continuous function is integrable, and so is every bounded function with only finitely many discontinuities. Module 19 defines integrability on a rectangle.

## integrand
links: 9.4#definite-integral
The function $f(x)$ in $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.

## limits of integration
links: 9.4#definite-integral
The numbers $a$ (lower) and $b$ (upper) in $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$: the ends of the interval, not limits in the sense of Module 2. Reversing them changes the sign of the integral, and $\int_c^c f(x)\,dx = 0$.

## net area
links: 9.4#signed-area
The area above the $x$-axis minus the area below it, between the graph of $f$ and the axis: what $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ measures. Also called signed area.

## total area
links: 9.4#signed-area
The area between a graph and the $x$-axis with every part counted as positive: the area above the axis plus the area below it, $\int_a^b \abs{f(x)}\,dx$.

## displacement
links: 9.4#signed-area 10.6#net-change-theorem 16.4#arithmetic
The net change in position, $s(b) - s(a) = \int_a^b v(t)\,dt$. In space, the vector from where something starts to where it ends.

## distance travelled
links: 9.4#signed-area
$\int_a^b \abs{v(t)}\,dt$: the distance a moving object covers, with every movement counted as positive.

## additivity over intervals
links: 9.5#properties 10.3#area-function
$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx + \int_b^c f(x)\,dx = \int_a^c f(x)\,dx$, when $f$ is continuous, or bounded with finitely many discontinuities, on an interval containing $a$, $b$ and $c$, in any order.

## comparison property
links: 9.5#properties
For $a < b$ and integrable $f$ and $g$: if $f \le g$ on $[a, b]$, then $\int_a^b f \le \int_a^b g$; and if $m \le f(x) \le M$ there, then $m(b - a) \le \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \le M(b - a)$.

## average value
links: 9.7#average 19.2#iterated
The average value of $f$ on $[a, b]$, where $a < b$, is $f_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. On a rectangle $R$ it is $\dfrac{1}{A(R)}\iint_R f\,dA$.

## Mean Value Theorem for integrals
links: 9.7#average
If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, where $a < b$, then $f(c) = \dfrac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ for some $c$ in $[a, b]$: $f$ takes its average value.

## Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
links: 9.8#faster-way 10.4#ftc1 10.5#ftc2
For $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$, Part 1 gives $A'(x)=f(x)$ for $A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ at interior points. Part 2 gives $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)$ if $F$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $F'=f$ on $(a,b)$.

## antiderivative
links: 10.1#antiderivatives
$F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ on an interval $I$ if $F'(x) = f(x)$ for every $x$ in $I$; at an endpoint that belongs to $I$, $F'$ is a one-sided derivative.

## general antiderivative
links: 10.1#antiderivatives
$F(x) + C$, where $F$ is one antiderivative of $f$ on an interval and $C$ stands for any constant: on an interval, these are all the antiderivatives of $f$.

## constant of integration
links: 10.1#antiderivatives
The arbitrary constant $C$ in a general antiderivative or an indefinite integral, $\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C$.

## power rule for antiderivatives
links: 10.1#table
$\int x^n\,dx = \dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n + 1} + C$ on $(0, \infty)$, for every real $n \ne -1$. For $n = -1$, $\int \dfrac{dx}{x} = \ln\abs{x} + C$ on any interval that does not contain 0.

## initial value problem
links: 10.2#initial-value 13.1#what
A differential equation together with initial conditions, such as $F'(x) = f(x)$ on an interval with $F(x_0) = y_0$. For a second-order equation the conditions give $y(t_0)$ and $y'(t_0)$.

## initial condition
links: 10.2#initial-value 13.1#what
A value that the solution of a differential equation must take at a given point, such as $y(t_0) = y_0$; a second-order equation also needs $y'(t_0) = v_0$.

## area function
links: 10.3#area-function
For $f$ integrable on every closed interval inside an interval $I$ and $a$ in $I$, $A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ for $x$ in $I$. Also called the accumulation function: it accumulates the signed area under $f$.

## indefinite integral
links: 10.6#indefinite
$\int f(x)\,dx$, the general antiderivative of $f$ on an interval: $\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C$ means that $F'(x) = f(x)$ on the interval. A definite integral is a number; an indefinite integral is a family of functions.

## net change theorem
links: 10.6#net-change-theorem
If $F'$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, then $\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$: the integral of a rate of change is the net change.

## power (physics)
links: 10.6#net-change-theorem
The rate at which energy is used or transferred, $P(t) = E'(t)$, in watts; its integral $\int_a^b P(t)\,dt$ is the energy used between $a$ and $b$.

## substitution rule
links: 10.7#substitution 10.7#definite-substitution
$\int f\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)\,dx = \int f(u)\,du$ with $u = g(x)$ and $du = g'(x)\,dx$: the chain rule in reverse. In a definite integral, $\int_a^b f\big(g(x)\big)g'(x)\,dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du$, for continuous $g'$ and $f$.

## error function
links: 10.8#special-functions 11.6#nonelementary 12.9#normal
$\operatorname{erf} x = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\displaystyle\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt$, for every real $x$: an antiderivative of $e^{-x^2}$ up to a constant factor, which has no elementary formula. Normal probabilities are computed with it.

## integration by parts
links: 11.1#parts 11.1#definite-parts
$\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du$, that is, $\int f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx = f(x)\,g(x) - \int f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx$, when $f$ and $g$ have continuous derivatives: the product rule in reverse. For definite integrals, $\int_a^b fg' = \big[fg\big]_a^b - \int_a^b f'g$.

## LIATE
links: 11.1#choosing
A rough guide to the choice of $u$ in integration by parts: take as $u$ the factor that comes first in the list Logarithmic, Inverse trigonometric, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential.

## tabular method
links: 11.1#repeated
A table that organises repeated integration by parts when $u$ is a polynomial: differentiate $u$ down one column until it reaches 0, integrate $dv$ down the other, and add the diagonal products with alternating signs.

## reduction formula
links: 11.1#reduction
A formula that expresses an integral with a whole-number parameter $n$ through the same integral with a smaller $n$, such as $\int \sin^n x\,dx = -\dfrac{\sin^{n-1}x\cos x}{n} + \dfrac{n - 1}{n}\int\sin^{n-2}x\,dx$ for $n \ge 2$.

## trigonometric integral
links: 11.2#trig-integrals
An integral of a product of powers of trigonometric functions, such as $\int \sin^m x\cos^n x\,dx$: save one factor of an odd power and substitute, or use the half-angle identities when both powers are even.

## half-angle identities
links: 11.2#trig-integrals
$\sin^2 x = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2x)$ and $\cos^2 x = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2x)$.

## trigonometric substitution
links: 11.3#trig-substitution
Substituting $x = a\sin\theta$, $a\tan\theta$ or $a\sec\theta$ (with $a > 0$) to remove $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, $\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}$ or $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$ from an integrand: the substitution rule read from right to left. A right triangle turns the answer back into $x$.

## partial fractions
links: 11.4#partial-fractions 11.4#repeated-factors
Writing a proper rational function as a sum of simpler fractions, one or more for each factor of the denominator: $\dfrac{A}{x - r}, \dots, \dfrac{A_k}{(x - r)^k}$ for a factor $(x - r)^k$, and $\dfrac{Bx + C}{x^2 + px + q}, \dots$ for an irreducible quadratic factor. The constants are unique.

## proper rational function
links: 11.4#partial-fractions
A rational function $P(x)/Q(x)$ in which the degree of $P$ is less than the degree of $Q$; otherwise it is improper, and long division turns it into a polynomial plus a proper one.

## cover-up method
links: 11.4#linear-factors
For $Q(x) = (x - r_1)\cdots(x - r_n)$ with different $r_k$ and $P$ of degree less than $n$: the constant over $x - r_k$ in the partial fractions of $P/Q$ is $P(r_k)$ divided by the product of the other factors at $r_k$, found by covering up $x - r_k$.

## irreducible quadratic
links: 11.4#repeated-factors
A quadratic $x^2 + px + q$ with $p^2 < 4q$ (a negative discriminant): it has no real zeros, so it has no real linear factors.

## strategy for integration
links: 11.5#strategy
Simplify the integrand; substitute if it contains a function with its derivative; classify it by its form (parts, trigonometric integral, trigonometric substitution, partial fractions, roots); and if that fails, try again, combining methods.

## elementary function
links: 11.6#nonelementary
A function built from constants and $x$ with finitely many additions, subtractions, multiplications, divisions, powers, roots, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric and inverse trigonometric functions, and compositions of these.

## Liouville's theorem
links: 11.6#nonelementary
A theorem (quoted, not proved, in this series) that describes what an elementary antiderivative must look like. From it, $e^{-x^2}$, $\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ and $\dfrac{1}{\ln x}$ have no elementary antiderivative.

## sine integral
links: 11.6#nonelementary
$\operatorname{Si} x = \displaystyle\int_0^x \frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt$, for every real $x$, with the integrand given the value 1 at $t = 0$.

## logarithmic integral
links: 11.6#nonelementary
$\operatorname{Li} x = \displaystyle\int_2^x \frac{dt}{\ln t}$, for $x > 1$.

## numerical integration
links: 11.7#numerical
Computing an integral approximately from values of the integrand, with a rule such as the midpoint, trapezoidal or Simpson's rule; needed when there is no elementary antiderivative or the integrand is known only through measurements.

## trapezoidal rule
links: 11.7#trapezoidal 11.7#error-bounds
$T_n = \dfrac{\Delta x}{2}\big[f(x_0) + 2f(x_1) + \dots + 2f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)\big]$, with $\Delta x = (b - a)/n$ and $x_i = a + i\,\Delta x$: the average of the left and right sums. If $f''$ is continuous and $\abs{f''} \le K$ on $[a, b]$, its error is at most $\dfrac{K(b - a)^3}{12n^2}$.

## Simpson's rule
links: 11.7#simpson
$S_n = \dfrac{\Delta x}{3}\big[f(x_0) + 4f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + \dots + 4f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)\big]$, for even $n$. It is exact for polynomials of degree at most 3, and if $f^{(4)}$ is continuous and $\abs{f^{(4)}} \le K$ on $[a, b]$, its error is at most $\dfrac{K(b - a)^5}{180n^4}$.

## adaptive rule
links: 11.7#which-rule
A numerical integration rule that keeps halving the subintervals only where the integrand changes fast, as library routines do.

## density
links: 12.1#method 19.5#mass
Mass per unit length of a rod, $\rho(x)$, or per unit area of a plate, $\rho(x, y)$. For probability densities, see probability density function.

## slice, approximate, add
links: 12.1#method
The way integrals arise in applications: slice $[a, b]$ into thin pieces, approximate the part of the quantity in each piece by $q(x)\,\Delta x$, add the pieces into a Riemann sum, and take the limit to get $\int_a^b q(x)\,dx$.

## area between curves
links: 12.2#area
For $f$ and $g$ continuous on $[a, b]$ with $g \le f$, the area of the region between their graphs is $\int_a^b \big(f(x) - g(x)\big)\,dx$.

## volume by slicing
links: 12.3#slicing
A solid between the planes $x = a$ and $x = b$ whose cross-section at $x$ has area $A(x)$, with $A$ continuous, has volume $\int_a^b A(x)\,dx$.

## solid of revolution
links: 12.3#washers
The solid swept out when a region of the plane rotates about a line in that plane, the axis.

## disc method
links: 12.3#washers
The volume of the solid swept out when the region under $y = f(x) \ge 0$, $a \le x \le b$, rotates about the $x$-axis: $V = \pi\int_a^b f(x)^2\,dx$, adding discs of radius $f(x)$.

## washer
links: 12.3#washers
A disc with a hole: the cross-section of a solid of revolution when the region does not reach the axis. With outer radius $R(x)$ and inner radius $r(x)$, the washer method gives $V = \pi\int_a^b \big(R(x)^2 - r(x)^2\big)\,dx$.

## outer radius
links: 12.3#washers
In the washer method, the distance $R(x)$ from the axis to the farther boundary of the region; the inner radius $r(x)$ is the distance to the nearer one.

## cylindrical shell
links: 12.4#shells
A thin tube of radius $x$, height $f(x)$ and thickness $\Delta x$, swept out when a thin strip rotates about the $y$-axis.

## shell method
links: 12.4#shells
When the region under the graph of a continuous $f \ge 0$, for $0 \le a \le x \le b$, rotates about the $y$-axis, the solid has volume $V = 2\pi\int_a^b x\,f(x)\,dx$; in general $2\pi\int (\text{radius})(\text{height})\,dx$.

## arc length
links: 12.5#arc-length 16.2#arc-length 16.8#integrals
The length of a curve: the limit of the lengths of inscribed polygons, if it exists. For $y = f(x)$ with $f'$ continuous on $[a, b]$, $L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2}\,dx$; for a path, $\int_a^b \norm{\vb{r}'(t)}\,dt$.

## work
links: 12.6#work 16.5#dot 20.2#work
A constant force $F$ acting through a distance $d$ does work $W = Fd$; a continuous force $F(x)$ moving an object along a line from $a$ to $b$ does work $\int_a^b F(x)\,dx$. For a force vector, $W = \vb{F}\cdot\vb{D}$, and along a curve the line integral $\int_C \vb{F}\cdot d\vb{r}$.

## joule
links: 12.6#work 16.5#work
The unit of work and energy: a force of 1 newton acting through 1 metre does 1 joule (J) of work.

## Hooke's law
links: 12.6#work 13.8#second-order
A spring stretched $x$ metres beyond its natural length pulls back with a force proportional to $x$: $F(x) = kx$, where the spring constant $k > 0$ is measured in N/m.

## spring constant
links: 12.6#work
The constant $k$ in Hooke's law $F = kx$, the spring's stiffness, in newtons per metre.

## centre of mass
links: 12.7#centre-of-mass 19.5#mass 19.6#triple
The point at which a body balances. Masses $m_i$ at $x_i$ on a line have $\bar x = \sum m_ix_i/\sum m_i$; a rod along $[a, b]$ with continuous density $\rho > 0$ has $\bar x = \dfrac1M\int_a^b x\,\rho(x)\,dx$, where $M = \int_a^b \rho(x)\,dx$. A plate has $(\bar x, \bar y) = (M_y/m, M_x/m)$.

## moment
links: 12.7#centre-of-mass 19.5#mass
The moment of masses $m_i$ at $x_i$ about the origin is $\sum m_ix_i$. A plate with density $\rho$ has moments $M_y = \iint_D x\rho\,dA$ about the $y$-axis and $M_x = \iint_D y\rho\,dA$ about the $x$-axis.

## lamina
links: 12.7#centre-of-mass 19.5#mass
A thin flat plate.

## centroid
links: 12.7#centre-of-mass 19.5#mass 19.6#triple
The centre of mass of a plate or solid of uniform positive density. For a plate between continuous $g\le f$ on $[a,b]$ with area $A>0$, $\bar x=\tfrac1A\int_a^b x(f-g)\,dx$ and $\bar y=\tfrac1A\int_a^b \tfrac12(f^2-g^2)\,dx$.

## improper integral
links: 12.8#improper 12.8#infinite-intervals 12.8#unbounded
An integral over an infinite interval, or of a function unbounded near a point of the interval, defined as a limit of ordinary integrals: $\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^t f(x)\,dx$, and so on. It converges if the limit exists as a finite number, and diverges otherwise.

## $p$-integrals
sort: p-integrals
links: 12.8#p-integrals
$\int_1^\infty \dfrac{dx}{x^p}$ converges, to $\dfrac{1}{p - 1}$, if $p > 1$, and diverges if $p \le 1$; $\int_0^1 \dfrac{dx}{x^p}$ converges, to $\dfrac{1}{1 - p}$, if $p < 1$, and diverges if $p \ge 1$.

## comparison test
links: 12.8#comparison 14.6#comparison
For integrals: if $0 \le f(x) \le g(x)$ for $x \ge a$, with $f$ and $g$ continuous, then $\int_a^\infty f$ converges if $\int_a^\infty g$ does, and $\int_a^\infty g$ diverges if $\int_a^\infty f$ does. For series: if $0 \le a_n \le b_n$ from some index on, $\sum a_n$ converges if $\sum b_n$ does, and $\sum b_n$ diverges if $\sum a_n$ does.

## limit comparison test
links: 12.8#comparison 14.6#limit-comparison
If $f$ and $g$ are continuous and positive with $f(x)/g(x) \to L$ as $x \to \infty$, where $0 < L < \infty$, then $\int_a^\infty f$ and $\int_a^\infty g$ both converge or both diverge. For series with $a_n > 0$, $b_n > 0$ and $a_n/b_n \to L$, $0 < L < \infty$: $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ both converge or both diverge.

## Gabriel's horn
links: 12.8#gabriel
The solid swept out when the graph of $y = 1/x$, $x \ge 1$, rotates about the $x$-axis: its volume is finite ($\pi$), but its surface area is infinite.

## probability
links: 12.9#densities
The probability of an event, $P(\text{event})$, is a number from 0 to 1: the fraction of the time the event happens in a long run of repetitions. 1 means certain.

## random variable
links: 12.9#densities 19.5#joint-densities
A quantity whose value is decided by chance. It is continuous if it has a probability density.

## probability density function
links: 12.9#densities
A function $f$ with $f(x) \ge 0$ for every $x$ and $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$. A random variable $X$ has density $f$ if $P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ for all $a \le b$.

## uniform density
links: 12.9#densities
The density on $[0, 1]$ equal to 1 there and 0 elsewhere: for $0 \le a \le b \le 1$, $P(a \le X \le b) = b - a$.

## expected value
links: 12.9#expected-value
The expected value, or mean, of a random variable $X$ with density $f$ is $\mu = E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f(x)\,dx$, when the integral converges.

## exponential distribution
links: 12.9#exponential-distribution
The density $f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \ge 0$ and 0 for $x < 0$, with rate $\lambda > 0$: a model for the waiting time for events that happen at random, at a steady average rate $\lambda$. Its mean is $1/\lambda$, and it has no memory: $P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)$.

## conditional probability
links: 12.9#exponential-distribution
$P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}$, for $P(B) > 0$: the probability that $A$ happens among the occasions on which $B$ happens.

## standard normal density
links: 12.9#normal
$\varphi(z) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-z^2/2}$, the bell-shaped density of statistics, with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

## normal distribution
links: 12.9#normal
The distribution with density $\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$, with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma > 0$. A normal random variable lies within one, two and three standard deviations of its mean about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of the time: the 68–95–99.7 rule.

## standard deviation
links: 12.9#normal ex12.12
The number $\sigma > 0$ that sets the width of a normal distribution; $\sigma^2$, the variance, is the mean squared distance from the mean.

## variance
links: ex12.12
The mean squared distance from the mean, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (x - \mu)^2f(x)\,dx$; for the normal distribution it is $\sigma^2$.

## Gaussian integral
links: 12.9#normal 19.4#gaussian
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt\pi$: quoted in Module 12, proved in Module 19 with a double integral in polar coordinates.

## torus
links: ex12.10
The shape of a ring doughnut: the solid swept out when a disc of radius $a$ rotates about a line in its plane at distance $R > a$ from its centre.

## ordinary differential equation
links: 13.1#what
A differential equation whose unknown is a function of one variable, usually time $t$.

## order (of a differential equation)
links: 13.1#what
The order of the highest derivative in the equation: $y' = ky$ is first order, $my'' + ky = 0$ second order.

## solution (of a differential equation)
links: 6.8#growth-decay 13.1#what
A function, on an open interval $I$, that has the derivatives the equation involves at every point of $I$ and makes the equation true for every $t$ in $I$.

## general solution
links: 13.1#what
A formula, with arbitrary constants, that gives every solution of a differential equation; any one solution is a particular solution.

## particular solution
links: 13.1#what
Any one solution of a differential equation, as opposed to the general solution, which gives them all.

## existence and uniqueness theorem
links: 13.1#existence 13.8#second-order
If $F$ and $F_y$ are continuous on an open rectangle containing $(t_0, y_0)$, then $y' = F(t, y)$, $y(t_0) = y_0$ has a solution on some open interval containing $t_0$, and only one there. The equation $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$, $a \ne 0$, has exactly one solution with given $y(t_0)$ and $y'(t_0)$, defined for every $t$.

## blow up
links: 13.1#existence
A solution blows up when it grows without bound as $t$ approaches a finite time: $y = 1/(1 - t)$, the solution of $y' = y^2$, $y(0) = 1$, blows up as $t \to 1$.

## model
links: 13.2#models
A simplified description of a real situation in mathematical terms, often a differential equation, good when its assumptions hold. Writing it down is modelling.

## Newton's law of cooling
links: 13.2#models 13.5#cooling
The rate at which an object's temperature $T$ changes is proportional to its difference from the constant temperature $T_s$ of its surroundings: $T' = -k(T - T_s)$ with $k > 0$. Its solution is $T(t) = T_s + \big(T(0) - T_s\big)e^{-kt}$.

## terminal velocity
links: 13.2#models
The constant speed $v_T = mg/c$ at which a body falling with air resistance $cv$ stops speeding up: its weight $mg$ and the resistance balance.

## direction field
links: 13.3#direction-fields
The picture of a first-order equation $y' = F(t, y)$: a short segment of slope $F(t, y)$ at each point of a grid. Solution curves are tangent to it. Also called a slope field.

## isocline
links: 13.3#direction-fields
A curve along which the slope $F(t, y)$ of a direction field is constant.

## autonomous
links: 13.3#equilibria
A differential equation $y' = f(y)$ whose right side does not depend on $t$.

## equilibrium solution
links: 13.3#equilibria
A constant solution $y = y^*$ of $y' = f(y)$: a number with $f(y^*) = 0$. It is stable if every solution that starts close enough to it approaches it as $t \to \infty$, and unstable if some solutions that start as close to it as you like move away. If $f'$ is continuous, $f'(y^*) < 0$ makes it stable and $f'(y^*) > 0$ unstable.

## phase line
links: 13.3#equilibria
A sketch of the $y$-axis with arrows, up where $f > 0$ and down where $f < 0$, which shows where every solution of $y' = f(y)$ goes.

## Euler's method
links: 13.4#euler 13.4#euler-error
A numerical method for $y' = F(t, y)$, $y(t_0) = y_0$: with a step size $h > 0$, compute $t_{n+1} = t_n + h$ and $y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,F(t_n, y_n)$, following the direction field in straight steps. It has order 1: its error at a fixed time is $O(h)$.

## Runge–Kutta method (RK4)
sort: Runge Kutta method
links: 13.4#rk4
The classical fourth-order method for $y' = F(t, y)$: each step averages four slopes, $y_{n+1} = y_n + \tfrac{h}{6}(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4)$. Its error at a fixed time is $O(h^4)$.

## stiff
links: 13.4#rk4
A differential equation with fast-decaying parts, which force ordinary methods to take tiny steps; numerical software has special methods for it.

## separable equation
links: 13.5#separable
A first-order equation that can be written $y' = g(t)\,h(y)$. Separating the variables, $\int \dfrac{dy}{h(y)} = \int g(t)\,dt$, gives its solutions where $h \ne 0$.

## time constant
links: 13.5#cooling 13.7#linear
For $y' = -k(y - A)$ with $k > 0$, the time $1/k$ in which the distance from the equilibrium $A$ shrinks by the factor $e$. For an RC circuit it is $\tau = RC$.

## logistic equation
links: 13.6#logistic
$y' = ry\Big(1 - \dfrac{y}{K}\Big)$, with constants $r > 0$ and $K > 0$: growth that is nearly exponential while $y$ is small and slows to 0 as $y$ approaches the carrying capacity $K$. Its solutions, $y = \dfrac{K}{1 + \frac{K - y_0}{y_0}e^{-rt}}$, trace S-shaped logistic curves.

## carrying capacity
links: 13.6#logistic
The level $K$ in the logistic equation at which the per-capita growth rate $y'/y$ falls to 0: a stable equilibrium that every positive population approaches.

## first-order linear equation
links: 13.7#linear
A first-order differential equation that can be written in the standard form $y' + p(t)\,y = q(t)$, where $p$ and $q$ are functions of $t$ alone.

## integrating factor
links: 13.7#linear
For $y' + p(t)y = q(t)$, the function $\mu = e^{P}$, where $P$ is an antiderivative of $p$: multiplying by it makes the left side $(\mu y)'$, so $y = \dfrac{1}{\mu}\Big(\int \mu q\,dt + C\Big)$.

## low-pass filter
links: 13.7#linear
A circuit, such as a resistor and a capacitor in series, that follows slow changes of its input and smooths out fast ones.

## linear homogeneous equation with constant coefficients
links: 13.8#second-order
A second-order equation $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$ with constants $a \ne 0$, $b$ and $c$. "Homogeneous" because the right side is 0.

## superposition
links: 13.8#second-order
If $y_1$ and $y_2$ solve $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$, so does $C_1y_1 + C_2y_2$ for all constants $C_1$ and $C_2$.

## characteristic equation
links: 13.8#second-order
$ar^2 + br + c = 0$, the equation for the numbers $r$ that make $y = e^{rt}$ a solution of $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$. Two real roots give the solutions $C_1e^{r_1t} + C_2e^{r_2t}$, one root gives $(C_1 + C_2t)e^{rt}$, and no real roots give $e^{\alpha t}(C_1\cos\beta t + C_2\sin\beta t)$ with $\alpha = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$ and $\beta = \dfrac{\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a}$.

## simple harmonic motion
links: 13.8#second-order
The undamped oscillation $x = C_1\cos\omega t + C_2\sin\omega t$, with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, of a mass $m$ on a spring of stiffness $k$: it goes on for ever with period $2\pi/\omega$.

## damping
links: 13.8#second-order
A resisting force $-cx'$ proportional to the velocity, with damping constant $c \ge 0$. A mass on a spring is overdamped if $c^2 > 4mk$, critically damped if $c^2 = 4mk$ (neither oscillates), and underdamped if $c^2 < 4mk$ (it oscillates with shrinking size).

## quadrature
links: 11.7#numerical
Numerical approximation of a definite integral, such as the midpoint, trapezoidal or Simpson rule.
