Module 10Structured proofs in Isar

Write proofs that a reader can follow line by line and Isabelle checks step by step. You will state each step of a proof in Isar, pass facts from one step to the next, calculate with chains of equations and inequalities, and split proofs into cases and induction steps.

⏱ 220 minYou need: Modules 1–9

In this module you will

  • Write a proof as a proof … qed block with assume, fix, have and show
  • Pass facts between steps with then, from, with, using, note, moreover and ultimately
  • Prove chains of equations and inequalities with also, finally and …
  • Split a proof into cases, and write inductions with named cases, ?case and the induction hypothesis
  • Turn natural-deduction proofs and apply scripts into Isar, and repair an Isar proof from Isabelle’s messages
1

Why structured proofs

In Module 7 you built proofs from apply steps, one rule at a time, and in Module 9 you let automation prove whole goals at once. Both work, and both have a weakness that shows once a proof grows beyond a few lines. Here is a proof in the style of Module 7. Read it, and ask yourself what is true after the fourth step.

The statement is about access control: if every administrator is a member of staff, and there is an administrator, then there is a member of staff. ∀u. admin u ⟶ staff u reads “for every user u, if u is an administrator then u is staff”.

Isabelle
lemma "(∀u. admin u ⟶ staff u) ⟶ (∃u. admin u) ⟶ (∃u. staff u)"
  apply (rule impI)
  apply (rule impI)
  apply (erule exE)
  apply (rule exI)
  apply (erule allE)
  apply (erule mp)
  apply assumption
  done

Isabelle accepts the script. But it records what Isabelle should do, not what is true. Which user does exI choose? Which assumption does erule allE take apart? The answers are in the proof states, and the proof states are not in the text: to understand the script you have to replay it in Isabelle and read the state after every line. The same holds for whoever maintains the proof later. If a change in the library makes one step leave its subgoals in a different order, a later step fails, far from the cause, and the script gives no hint of what was intended.

Here is the same theorem proved in Isar:

Isabelle
lemma "(∀u. admin u ⟶ staff u) ⟶ (∃u. admin u) ⟶ (∃u. staff u)"
proof (intro impI)
  assume all: "∀u. admin u ⟶ staff u"
  assume "∃u. admin u"
  then obtain a where "admin a" by blast
  with all have "staff a" by blast
  then show "∃u. staff u" by blast
qed

You have not met these keywords yet, and you can probably read the proof all the same, because it says what holds at each step. It is the paper proof, line for line: assume that every administrator is staff, and that there is an administrator. Call one of them a. By the first assumption, a is staff. So there is a member of staff.

Isar is Isabelle’s language for such structured proofs; the name stands for Intelligible semi-automated reasoning. An Isar proof is a text made of statements. Each step claims a fact, and Isabelle checks that the claim follows, usually with a short call to automation such as by blast. Three things follow from this design.

  • You can read the proof without running it. The facts are in the text, not hidden in proof states.
  • A failing step is marked where it fails. Its statement tells you what was meant, so you know what to repair.
  • It matches paper proofs. The natural-deduction proofs of Module 6 and the induction proofs of Module 5 turn into Isar almost line for line (Section 10.7).

Isabelle’s own tutorial, Programming and Proving in Isabelle/HOL, recommends Isar for larger proofs, and the rest of this series writes its longer proofs in it. Apply scripts stay useful for exploring a goal step by step, and short proofs stay by simp or by auto.

Apply script Isar proof
Each line says which method to apply next which fact holds next
To follow it you need Isabelle, to see each state the text
Its shape a list of steps nested blocks, like a program
Key idea

An apply script tells Isabelle what to do to the goal. An Isar proof tells the reader what is true at each step, and Isabelle checks every claim.

The shape of an Isar proof

An Isar proof is built from a handful of commands. This section introduces them on small examples from logic, so that you can concentrate on the structure, and then on a first function from programming.

Proof blocks

You know two ways to finish a lemma: a single step by method, and an apply script that ends with done. Isar adds a third, the proof block:

Isabellenot checked
lemma "statement"
proof method
  steps
qed

The method after proof makes the first move on the goal, as apply would. The steps between proof and qed then prove what is left, and qed closes the block.

Definition 10.1Proof block

A proof block proof m … qed proves the goal stated directly before it. The initial method m turns the goal into pending goals. The steps inside the block establish facts and solve the pending goals; qed closes the block once all of them are solved. Every claim inside the block needs a proof of its own, which is again either a single step by m or a proof block, so blocks nest.

Take the formula you proved by hand in Module 6 and with apply in Module 7: A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A. State it:

Isabelle
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A

Start the block with the rule for implication from Module 7:

Isabelle
proof (rule impI)
Output
proof (state)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ∧ B ⟹ B ∧ A

The goal now says: assuming A ∧ B, show B ∧ A. The arrow ⟹ is Isabelle’s own implication, which you met in Module 7. In a paper proof you would now write “Assume A ∧ B”. In Isar too:

Isabelle
  assume ab: "A ∧ B"
Output
proof (state)
this:
  A ∧ B

goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ∧ B ⟹ B ∧ A

assume adds the assumption to the proof and calls it ab. The state lists it under this:, the most recent fact. The goal has not changed: an assumption proves nothing by itself, it is something you may use. Now claim the conclusion:

Isabelle
  show "B ∧ A"
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. B ∧ A

show states a claim that solves a pending goal. Like a lemma, the claim needs a proof, and the state shows it as a goal of its own. Prove it with blast, giving it the assumption, and close the block:

Isabelle
    using ab by blast
qed

using ab hands the fact ab to blast; Section 10.3.3 explains why you have to. When the proof of the show succeeds, Isabelle uses the shown fact to solve the pending goal, and qed finds nothing left to do. This works because ab is the premise A ∧ B of the goal. An assume must match a premise of the goal: assume something else, and the show at the end fails (Section 10.8).

Good to know

The first line of every proof state names the mode Isabelle is in, which tells you what may come next. proof (prove): a claim (lemma, have, show) has been stated, and its proof comes next. proof (state): you are inside a block and may assume, claim or close it. proof (chain): facts have been picked up for the next claim (Section 10.3).

Steps on the way: have

A paper proof rarely jumps to its conclusion: it reaches it through intermediate facts. have states such a fact, with its own proof, and adds it to the proof for later steps. Unlike show, have does not touch the pending goals.

Isabelle
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
proof (rule impI)
  assume ab: "A ∧ B"
  have b: "B" using ab by blast
  have a: "A" using ab by blast
  show "B ∧ A" using b a by blast
qed

Each have gets a name, b and a, and the show uses both. The proof reads as on paper: from A ∧ B we get B, and we get A, so B ∧ A.

Nested blocks and next

The proof of a have or a show can itself be a block. Here the conclusion B ∧ A is proved with the rule for conjunction, which leaves two goals:

Isabelle
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
proof (rule impI)
  assume ab: "A ∧ B"
  show "B ∧ A"
  proof (rule conjI)
Output
proof (state)
goal (2 subgoals):
 1. B
 2. A

Each of the two goals gets its own show:

Isabelle
    show "B" using ab by blast
  next
    show "A" using ab by blast
  qed
qed

A show may solve any pending goal of its block, not only the first: Isabelle looks for a goal that the shown fact solves. next separates the proofs of different goals. It closes the current part of the block and opens a fresh one, so that whatever you assume for one goal is not available for the next. Here nothing is assumed inside the inner block, so next could be left out. In the case analysis and induction patterns below (Sections 10.5 and 10.6), use it to separate the case contexts. Figure 10.1 shows the structure of this proof.

Arbitrary values: fix

To prove a statement for all x, a paper proof says “let x be arbitrary” and proves the statement for that x. This is the rule (∀I) of Module 6. Isar says fix x:

Isabelle
lemma "∀u. admin u ⟶ admin u ∨ staff u"
proof
Output
proof (state)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. ⋀u. admin u ⟶ admin u ∨ staff u

proof without a method applied the rule for ∀ (Section 10.2.6 lists what it does for each connective). The goal starts with ⋀u, Isabelle’s own “for every u” from Module 7. Fix such a u and prove the statement for it:

Isabelle
  fix u
  show "admin u ⟶ admin u ∨ staff u"
  proof
    assume a: "admin u"
    show "admin u ∨ staff u" using a by blast
  qed
qed
Definition 10.2Fixed variable

fix x introduces a fixed variable x, like the variables bound by ⋀ in Module 7: a name for an arbitrary value, about which the proof knows nothing except its type and the available assumptions. On leaving the block, Isabelle generalises x and discharges local assumptions: proving P x under A x establishes ⋀x. A x ⟹ P x, not an unconditional ⋀x. P x.

Lemmas with assumptions: assumes, shows and ?thesis

Statements about programs usually have conditions. Here is a function that every programmer has written in some form: clamp lo hi x keeps a value x inside the range from lo to hi, as a volume control or a sensor reading does.

Isabelle
fun clamp :: "int ⇒ int ⇒ int ⇒ int" where
  "clamp lo hi x = (if x < lo then lo else if hi < x then hi else x)"
Output
consts
  clamp :: "int ⇒ int ⇒ int ⇒ int"

Found termination order: "{}"
Isabelle
value "clamp 0 100 150"
value "clamp 0 100 (-5)"
value "clamp 0 100 42"
Output
"100"
  :: "int"

"0"
  :: "int"

"42"
  :: "int"

The result is never below lo, provided the range is not empty. A lemma can state the condition separately, with assumes, and the claim with shows:

Isabelle
lemma clamp_lower:
  assumes "lo ≤ hi"
  shows "lo ≤ clamp lo hi x"
Output
proof (prove)
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. lo ≤ clamp lo hi x

The goal shows only the claim. The assumption is available as a fact called assms, the list of all assumptions of the lemma:

Isabelle
proof -
  show ?thesis using assms by simp
qed

Two new things appear here. proof - starts a block without changing the goal: the hyphen is the method that does nothing. And ?thesis, read “the thesis”, is an abbreviation that Isabelle defines for every claim: it stands for the statement after shows (or after lemma, have or show), here lo ≤ clamp lo hi x. It saves you from copying long statements. You can also name each assumption, assumes order: "lo ≤ hi", or refer to the i-th one as assms(i). The result of the lemma is the implication lo ≤ hi ⟹ lo ≤ clamp lo hi x, which later proofs use under the name clamp_lower.

A one-step proof works too: using assms by simp directly after the statement. The block form pays off when the proof needs several steps.

How a block starts

The initial method decides which pending goals the block has to prove. Three forms cover most proofs:

  • proof (rule r) applies the rule r backwards, as apply (rule r) does, for example rule impI.
  • proof with no method applies one standard rule chosen by the main connective of the goal (or, when facts are passed to it, an elimination rule for them; Section 10.5.3).
  • proof - leaves the goal as it is.

For the standard rule, the block then follows the patterns of natural deduction:

Goal proof applies Inside the block
A ⟶ B impI assume "A" … show "B"
A ∧ B conjI show "A" … next … show "B"
A ⟷ B iffI assume "A" … show "B" next assume "B" … show "A"
¬ A notI assume "A" … show False
∀x. P x allI fix x … show "P x"
∃x. P x exI show "P t" for a witness t of your choice
Watch out

proof on its own fails when no standard rule fits the goal, for example when the goal is False or an equation between numbers:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma
  assumes "A" and "¬ A"
  shows False
proof
  show False using assms by blast
qed
Output
Failed to apply initial proof method:
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. False

The fix is proof -, which starts the block without applying any rule:

Isabelle
lemma
  assumes "A" and "¬ A"
  shows False
proof -
  show False using assms by blast
qed

Passing facts along

The proofs so far handed facts to methods with using. Isar has several ways to pass facts from one step to the next. They make the flow of a proof visible, as words like “hence” and “from this” do on paper.

Chaining facts with then

Definition 10.3Fact, this and chaining

A fact is a proved statement available in a proof: an assumption, the result of have or show, or a lemma from the library or from your theory. After each step, the fact it produced is called this. Forward chaining passes facts to the next claim, whose proof method receives them, as using does.

then chains this into the next claim. A proof in which every step uses the result of the step before reads like a pipeline:

Isabelle
lemma "A ∧ (A ⟶ B) ⟶ B ∨ C"
proof (rule impI)
  assume "A ∧ (A ⟶ B)"
  then have "B"
Output
proof (chain)
picking this:
  A ∧ (A ⟶ B)

proof (prove)
using this:
  A ∧ (A ⟶ B)

goal (1 subgoal):
 1. B

Isabelle prints two states for the last line, one for each command. After then the mode is proof (chain), and picking this shows the fact being passed. After have "B" the new goal appears, with the fact it will use listed under using this. In Isabelle/jEdit you see the state of the command under the cursor. Finish the pipeline:

Isabelle
    by blast
  then show "B ∨ C" by blast
qed

Names, from, with, using and note

A pipeline breaks as soon as a step needs an older fact. from names the facts to pass, so then is the same as from this. Facts can be referred to by name, or by their statement between the quotes ‹ and ›: ‹A› reads “the fact A” and refers to the fact of the proof whose statement is A.

Isabelle
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
proof (rule impI)
  assume ab: "A ∧ B"
  from ab have "A" by blast
  from ab have "B" by blast
  with ‹A› show "B ∧ A" by blast
qed

with ‹A› is short for from ‹A› this: it passes the named fact and the current one, here B.

Tip

To type ‹ and › in Isabelle/jEdit, type a back-quote (`) and accept the first completion, which inserts ‹› with the cursor between the two quotes.

note puts facts into this without proving anything, and can give the group a name:

Isabelle
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
proof (rule impI)
  assume ab: "A ∧ B"
  from ab have a: "A" by blast
  from ab have b: "B" by blast
  note both = b a
Output
proof (state)
this:
    B
    A

goal (1 subgoal):
 1. A ∧ B ⟹ B ∧ A
Isabelle
  from both show "B ∧ A" by (rule conjI)
qed

The order of the facts matters here: rule conjI uses the first fact for the left conjunct and the second for the right one. blast, simp and auto do not assign these positional roles to the facts. Their search or rewriting can still depend on fact order; there is no general promise that reordering facts leaves automation’s behaviour unchanged.

Fact 10.1Chaining shorthands
You write It means
then from this
with a from a this
have "P" using a from a have "P"
hence then have
thus then show

You will meet hence and thus in older proofs, including the Isabelle tutorial. Isabelle2026 still accepts them, but its reference manual calls them left-overs from the early history of Isar and recommends the longer forms, which this series uses.

Passing local facts to methods

In Section 10.2.5 the proof of clamp_lower said using assms. Leave it out:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma
  assumes "lo ≤ hi"
  shows "lo ≤ clamp lo hi x"
  by simp
Output
Failed to finish proof⌂:
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. hi < x ⟶ ¬ x < lo ⟶ lo ≤ hi

The simplifier unfolded clamp and split the ifs, and what remains is the one case that needs the assumption: when x is above the range, clamp returns hi, and lo ≤ hi is exactly what simp did not know. The assumption was available as a local fact, but these methods do not automatically use every local fact. They receive the goal and facts passed with then, from, with or using, and consult their configured context rules (for simp, the simp rules, including the equation of clamp). A fact registered with [simp] can therefore be used without being passed explicitly. Premises already inside the subgoal are also available. Explicit fact passing makes the dependencies of these proofs visible.

Key idea

Local assumptions and proved facts are available to pass to a method; availability alone does not add them to its input or rule collections. When a step fails, first check that it receives the facts it needs.

Using an existential: obtain

The rule (∃E) of Module 6 says: from ∃x. P x you may introduce a new name a with P a, provided the conclusion does not mention a. In Isar this is obtain. Look again at the Isar proof of Section 10.1:

  • assume all: … and assume "∃u. admin u" take the two premises that proof (intro impI) left.
  • then obtain a where "admin a" by blast introduces a new fixed variable a and the fact admin a. The proof, by blast, shows that such an a exists, using the chained fact ∃u. admin u.
  • with all have "staff a" passes all and admin a to blast.
  • then show "∃u. staff u" uses staff a: a is the witness.

Forget to chain the existential, and obtain cannot be proved:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "(∃u. admin u) ⟶ (∃u. admin u ∨ staff u)"
proof
  assume "∃u. admin u"
  obtain a where "admin a" by blast
  then show "∃u. admin u ∨ staff u" by blast
qed
Output
Failed to apply initial proof method⌂:
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. (⋀a. admin a ⟹ thesis) ⟹ thesis

The goal in the message is what obtain has to prove: whatever you want to show (thesis), if it follows from admin a for an arbitrary a, then it holds. blast cannot prove that without the fact ∃u. admin u. Write then obtain, or name the fact and write obtain a where "admin a" using ex by blast with using after the clause.

Collecting facts: moreover and ultimately

Sometimes a step needs several facts that are proved one after another. Instead of naming each one, you can collect them: moreover adds the most recent fact, this, to a collection, and ultimately adds the last one and passes the whole collection to the next claim.

Isabelle
lemma clamp_in_range:
  assumes "lo ≤ hi"
  shows "lo ≤ clamp lo hi x ∧ clamp lo hi x ≤ hi"
proof -
  have "lo ≤ clamp lo hi x" using assms by simp
  moreover have "clamp lo hi x ≤ hi" using assms by simp
  ultimately show ?thesis by blast
qed

The proof says what a reviewer wants to see: the result is at least lo, it is at most hi, so it lies in the range.

4

Calculations

Much of mathematics, and much reasoning about programs, is calculation: a chain of equations or inequalities in which each step follows from the one before.

Chains of equations

On paper, you multiply out (a + b)(a + b) like this:

(a+b)(a+b)=a(a+b)+b(a+b)=a⋅a+a⋅b+(b⋅a+b⋅b)=a⋅a+2ab+b⋅b\begin{aligned} (a + b)(a + b) &= a(a + b) + b(a + b) \\ &= a \cdot a + a \cdot b + (b \cdot a + b \cdot b) \\ &= a \cdot a + 2ab + b \cdot b \end{aligned} (a+b)(a+b)​=a(a+b)+b(a+b)=a⋅a+a⋅b+(b⋅a+b⋅b)=a⋅a+2ab+b⋅b​

and conclude that the first term equals the last. Isar writes the same chain:

Isabelle
lemma "(a + b) * (a + b) = a * a + 2 * a * b + b * (b :: int)"
proof -
  have "(a + b) * (a + b) = a * (a + b) + b * (a + b)" by (rule distrib_right)
  also have "… = a * a + a * b + (b * a + b * b)" by (simp add: distrib_left)
  also have "… = a * a + 2 * a * b + b * b" by (simp add: algebra_simps)
  finally show ?thesis .
qed
  • … reads “the right-hand side of the previous step”. To type it, type three dots ...: Isabelle/jEdit offers the symbol …, and the three dots work as well.
  • also adds the new step to the chain. Isabelle composes the steps by transitivity: from a = b and b = c it derives a = c.
  • finally composes the last step and passes the result, first term = last term, to the next claim.
  • . (a single dot) directly uses the passed fact when it matches the claim, without deriving a weaker consequence by logical search. Here the claim is ?thesis, the statement of the lemma.

Each step has its own justification: distrib_right is the law (a + b)c = ac + bc, distrib_left the law a(b + c) = ab + ac, and algebra_simps a collection of laws such as commutativity, which simp uses to rearrange terms.

Definition 10.4Calculation

A calculation is a chain of steps t₁ R t₂, t₂ R t₃, …, each with its own proof, from which Isabelle derives t₁ R tₙ by transitivity: a R b and b R c give a R c. In Isar, also extends the chain and finally ends it; … stands for the right-hand side of the previous step.

Inequalities

Chains may mix =, ≤ and <. The result has the strictest relation of the chain: one < anywhere makes the whole chain <.

Example 10.1Indices in a binary heap

A binary heap is often stored in an array: the node at index i has its left child at index 2i + 1. If i is one of the first n indices, is the child within the first 2n?

Isabelle
lemma heap_child:
  fixes i n :: nat
  assumes "i < n"
  shows "2 * i + 1 < 2 * n"
proof -
  have "2 * i + 1 < 2 * i + 2" by simp
  also have "… = 2 * (i + 1)" by simp
  also have "… ≤ 2 * n" using assms by simp
  finally show ?thesis .
qed

fixes i n :: nat declares the lemma’s variables with their type, for the whole statement; without it, Isabelle would not know which kind of numbers i and n are. The chain is < then = then ≤, so the result is 2 * i + 1 < 2 * n. Only the last step needs the assumption, and only that step is given it.

Watch out

For these numeric chains, finally produces the strictest relation, and . directly uses that fact. It does not automatically weaken < to ≤. A weaker claim fails, even though it follows:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma
  fixes x y z :: nat
  assumes "x < y" and "y ≤ z"
  shows "x ≤ z"
proof -
  have "x < y" using assms by simp
  also have "… ≤ z" using assms by simp
  finally show "x ≤ z" .
qed
Output
Failed to apply initial proof method:
using this:
  x < z
goal (1 subgoal):
 1. x ≤ z

The message shows the fact the chain produced (using this: x < z) and the claim. Either claim x < z, or replace . by a method that can weaken it, here by simp.

Chains with ≥ and > do not work as you might expect. Isabelle reads a ≥ b as b ≤ a, so … stands for a, not b, and the steps no longer fit together:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma
  fixes a b c :: nat
  assumes "a ≥ b" and "b ≥ c"
  shows "a ≥ c"
proof -
  have "a ≥ b" using assms by simp
  also have "… ≥ c" using assms by simp
  finally show "a ≥ c" .
qed
Output
Vacuous calculation result: (⋀x y. x ≤ y ⟹ a ≤ a) ⟹ b ≤ a
derived as projection (1) from:
    b ≤ a
    c ≤ a

Write chains from the small end to the big end, with ≤ and <.

5

Case analysis

Many proofs about programs split into cases: the list is empty or not, the condition of an if holds or not, the input is below, inside or above a range. In Isar each case gets its own part of the block, separated by next.

Two cases: true or false

clamp lo hi x should agree with the mathematical description “the maximum of lo and the minimum of hi and x”. Prove it by cases on the first test of clamp:

Isabelle
lemma clamp_max_min:
  assumes "lo ≤ hi"
  shows "clamp lo hi x = max lo (min hi x)"
proof (cases "x < lo")
Output
proof (state)
goal (2 subgoals):
 1. x < lo ⟹ clamp lo hi x = max lo (min hi x)
 2. ¬ x < lo ⟹ clamp lo hi x = max lo (min hi x)

proof (cases "x < lo") splits the goal into the case where x < lo holds and the case where it does not. The command case True enters the first case:

Isabelle
  case True
Output
proof (state)
this:
  x < lo

goal (2 subgoals):
 1. x < lo ⟹ clamp lo hi x = max lo (min hi x)
 2. ¬ x < lo ⟹ clamp lo hi x = max lo (min hi x)

case True assumes the condition of the case, which the state lists under this:. The state still lists both pending goals, and the claim to prove is still ?thesis, the statement of the lemma. Shown under the assumption x < lo, it solves the first goal:

Isabelle
  then show ?thesis using assms by simp
next
  case False
  then show ?thesis using assms by simp
qed

When you write proof (cases …) or proof (induction …), Isabelle/jEdit also shows a message that begins Proof outline with cases:, with the skeleton of the rest of the proof. For this lemma it is

Proof outline with cases:
  case True
  then show ?thesis sorry
next
  case False
  then show ?thesis sorry
qed

Click on the skeleton and Isabelle/jEdit inserts it into your text. Each sorry marks a proof still to be written: Isabelle/jEdit accepts it in place of a proof while you work, but a theory that contains sorry is not proved. Replace every one.

Cases of a datatype

For a list, the cases are the two ways of building one: the empty list [], called Nil, and y # ys, called Cons. Take a function that returns the first element of a list, or a default value d for the empty list:

Isabelle
fun first_or :: "'a ⇒ 'a list ⇒ 'a" where
  "first_or d [] = d"
| "first_or d (y # ys) = y"

Cases from a disjunction

The rule (∨E) of Module 6 proves C from A ∨ B by proving C once from A and once from B. In Isar, pass the disjunction to proof and give each case its assume. Here a user may read a file if they own it, or if they are an administrator:

Isabelle
lemma
  assumes who: "owner u ∨ admin u"
    and owners: "∀v. owner v ⟶ may_read v"
    and admins: "∀v. admin v ⟶ may_read v"
  shows "may_read u"
  using who
proof
  assume "owner u"
  with owners show ?thesis by blast
next
  assume "admin u"
  with admins show ?thesis by blast
qed

using who before proof passes the disjunction to the initial method, so proof applies the elimination rule for ∨ instead of an introduction rule. In both cases ?thesis is the claim of the lemma, may_read u.

Your own cases: consider

Sometimes the natural cases are neither a datatype nor a single condition. clamp distinguishes three: x below the range, inside it, or above it. consider states such a split, with a name for each case, and proves that the cases cover every possibility:

Isabelle
lemma
  assumes "lo ≤ hi"
  shows "lo ≤ clamp lo hi x ∧ clamp lo hi x ≤ hi"
proof -
  consider (below) "x < lo" | (inside) "lo ≤ x" "x ≤ hi" | (above) "hi < x"
    by linarith
  then show ?thesis
  proof cases
    case below
    then show ?thesis using assms by simp
  next
    case inside
    then show ?thesis by simp
  next
    case above
    then show ?thesis using assms by simp
  qed
qed

The proof of consider, here linarith from Module 9, shows that every x falls into one of the three cases. then show ?thesis proof cases then splits the proof along them, and case inside assumes both facts of that case. A lemma can state such a split for later use, with obtains in place of shows; in a proof, consider is the local form.

Induction with named cases

In Module 5 you proved properties of recursive functions with apply (induction xs) and read the base case and the induction step off the proof state. In Isar, each case of the induction becomes a named part of the block, and the induction hypothesis becomes a named fact.

The skeleton of an induction

Here is the total of a list of amounts, such as the items of a bill:

Isabelle
fun mysum :: "nat list ⇒ nat" where
  "mysum [] = 0"
| "mysum (x # xs) = x + mysum xs"

Found termination order: "size_list size <*mlex*> {}"

The total of two bills put together is the sum of their totals. Start an induction on xs:

case (Cons x xs) does three things. It fixes x and xs, your names for the variables the goal calls a and xs. It assumes the induction hypothesis, which the state shows under this:. And it sets ?case, read “the case”, to the claim of this case: mysum ((x # xs) @ ys) = mysum (x # xs) + mysum ys. Pass the hypothesis to simp with then:

Isabelle
  then show ?case by simp
qed
Definition 10.5Named case

In a proof by cases or induction, case c enters the case named c, and case (c x y …) also gives names to its variables. The case assumes its facts, available together under the name c. In an induction, case also sets ?case to the claim of the case, c.IH is the induction hypothesis and c.prems are the premises of the claim (Section 10.6.2). In a proof by cases, every case proves the same claim, ?thesis.

For natural numbers the cases are called 0 and Suc: case 0, then case (Suc n) with the hypothesis Suc.IH. For a datatype of your own they are named after its constructors.

The induction hypothesis, premises and ?case

A paper proof of the induction step is a calculation that uses the induction hypothesis once. The Isar proof can say exactly that, with the hypothesis named Cons.IH:

Isabelle
lemma "mysum (xs @ ys) = mysum xs + mysum ys"
proof (induction xs)
  case Nil
  show ?case by simp
next
  case (Cons x xs)
  have "mysum ((x # xs) @ ys) = x + mysum (xs @ ys)" by simp
  also have "… = x + (mysum xs + mysum ys)" using Cons.IH by simp
  also have "… = mysum (x # xs) + mysum ys" by simp
  finally show ?case .
qed

When the claim has a premise, the induction keeps it apart from the hypothesis.

Example 10.2A bill whose items are bounded

If every item of a bill costs at most m, the total is at most m times the number of items. set xs is the set of the elements of xs, so ∀x ∈ set xs. x ≤ m says that every item is at most m.

Isabelle
lemma mysum_bound: "(∀x ∈ set xs. x ≤ m) ⟹ mysum xs ≤ length xs * m"
proof (induction xs)
  print_cases
Output
cases:
  Nil:
    let "?case" = "mysum [] ≤ length [] * m"
    assume prems : "∀x∈set []. x ≤ m"
  Cons:
    fix a_ xs_
    let "?case" = "mysum (a_ # xs_) ≤ length (a_ # xs_) * m"
    assume IH : "∀x∈set xs_. x ≤ m ⟹ mysum xs_ ≤ length xs_ * m" and
      prems : "∀x∈set (a_ # xs_). x ≤ m"

print_cases lists the cases with everything they provide: the fixed variables, ?case, the induction hypothesis under IH and the premise of the claim under prems. The names a_ and xs_, with a trailing underscore, are hidden: the proof cannot refer to them until case (Cons a xs) gives them names. Each case receives the premise for its own list, and the hypothesis is the whole implication for the shorter list xs.

Isabelle
  case Nil
  then show ?case by simp
next
  case (Cons a xs)
  have "mysum (a # xs) = a + mysum xs" by simp
  also have "… ≤ m + mysum xs" using Cons.prems by simp
  also have "… ≤ m + length xs * m" using Cons.IH Cons.prems by simp
  also have "… = length (a # xs) * m" by simp
  finally show ?case .
qed

The second step needs only the premise: a ≤ m, because a is in the list. The third step needs the hypothesis, and the hypothesis needs its own premise, that every item of xs is at most m, which again follows from Cons.prems. So both are passed.

You have now seen the core Isar forms used in this series. This section puts them side by side with the proofs of Modules 5, 6 and 7, so that you can translate in both directions.

A natural-deduction proof

In Module 6 you proved A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A with this tree:

[A ∧ B]¹
∧E₂
B
[A ∧ B]¹
∧E₁
A
∧I
B ∧ A
⟶I¹
A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A

In Module 7 you replayed it with apply:

Isabelle
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
  apply (rule impI)
  apply (erule conjE)
  apply (rule conjI)
  apply assumption
  apply assumption
  done

The Isar proof follows the tree rule by rule. It needs (∧E₁) and (∧E₂) in the form the tree uses them, from A ∧ B to A and to B: the destruction rules conjunct1 and conjunct2 of Module 7.

Isabelle
thm conjunct1 conjunct2
Output
  ?P ∧ ?Q ⟹ ?P
  ?P ∧ ?Q ⟹ ?Q
Isabelle
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
proof (rule impI)
  assume ab: "A ∧ B"
  from ab have b: "B" by (rule conjunct2)
  from ab have a: "A" by (rule conjunct1)
  from b a show "B ∧ A" by (rule conjI)
qed

Each rule of the tree is one line of the proof (Figure 10.2). The assumption [A ∧ B]¹, which the tree discharges with (⟶I), is the assume inside the block that rule impI opened. The tree is read from the leaves down, the Isar text from the top down, and both contain the same steps. The apply script, in contrast, works backwards from the goal and never states B or A at all.

Writing the rule names is useful for learning. In practice you let Isabelle choose the rule: .. (two dots) is a proof by one standard rule step, so from ab have "B" .. works as well, and by blast proves such steps too.

From an apply script to Isar: a recipe

To turn an apply script into Isar, run the script in Isabelle/jEdit and read the proof state after each line. Then write down what the states say, not what the methods do:

  1. The first steps usually only introduce: rule impI, rule allI, intro impI. They become proof (intro …) or proof, followed by assume for each premise and fix for each ⋀-variable that the state then shows.
  2. A step that derives a new fact, such as drule mp or erule conjE, becomes a have with that fact as its statement.
  3. The step that finishes the goal becomes show.
  4. Prove each step with the rule of the apply step, or with automation.

Here is a script in the style of Module 7, for the transitivity of implication:

Isabelle
lemma "(A ⟶ B) ⟶ (B ⟶ C) ⟶ A ⟶ C"
  apply (intro impI)
  apply (erule mp)
  apply (erule mp)
  apply assumption
  done

After intro impI the state has the premises A ⟶ B, B ⟶ C and A and the conclusion C. The first erule mp works backwards from C to B, the second from B to A. Written forwards, as a reader thinks:

Isabelle
lemma "(A ⟶ B) ⟶ (B ⟶ C) ⟶ A ⟶ C"
proof (intro impI)
  assume ab: "A ⟶ B" and bc: "B ⟶ C" and a: "A"
  from ab a have b: "B" by (rule mp)
  from bc b show "C" by (rule mp)
qed

When an Isar proof fails

Because every step of an Isar proof is a statement, Isabelle can tell you which step failed and what remained of it. These are the messages you will meet most often; you have seen most of them in this module already.

Message Meaning What to do
Failed to finish proof, or Failed to apply initial proof method, at a by The method could not prove this step from the facts it was given. The message shows what remained. Pass the missing facts (then, using); add a lemma (simp add:); split the step; try sledgehammer (Module 9).
Failed to apply initial proof method, at proof The initial method cannot start, for example no standard rule fits the goal. Use proof -, or name the rule.
Failed to refine any pending goal The shown fact, under the assumptions of its block, solves no pending goal. Compare the claim with the goal in the state; check ?case and ?thesis, next, and assumptions that are not premises of the goal.
Illegal application of proof command in “prove” mode A claim has no proof, so the next command comes too early. Give the claim a proof, by … or proof … qed.
Undefined case, Undefined fact A case or fact name is wrong. Use the proof outline or print_cases.
Vacuous calculation result The steps of a chain do not fit together. Write chains with =, ≤ and <.

The fourth message appears, for example, when a have has no proof:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
proof (rule impI)
  assume ab: "A ∧ B"
  have "B"
  show "B ∧ A" using ab by blast
qed
Output
Illegal application of proof command in "prove" mode

After have "B" Isabelle is in prove mode and expects a proof; show is not one.

“Failed to refine any pending goal” deserves a closer look, because the step itself succeeded: the claim was proved, but it does not fit the goal. Isabelle/jEdit warns about this before the proof of the show has finished, with the message “Local statement fails to refine any pending goal”. Here is the typical case, a claim that is not the goal:

Isabellefails on purpose
lemma "A ∧ B ⟶ B ∧ A"
proof (rule impI)
  assume "A ∧ B"
  then show "A ∧ B" by blast
qed
Output
Failed to refine any pending goal

To find out what is wrong, change show into have: if the step is then accepted, the claim is right and the mismatch is between the claim and the goal. Look at the goal in the state and compare.

A few habits make Isar proofs easier to write and to keep working:

  • Write the skeleton first. State the lemma, write proof …, the assume, fix and show lines and qed, and let the proof outline fill in cases. Then prove the steps, one at a time.
  • One idea per step. If a by needs a long list of facts or a clever method, put an intermediate have in between.
  • Chain what you need and nothing else. Pass a fact where it is used, with then, with or using; name it only if it is used further away.
  • Let automation do the small steps. simp, blast, auto and linarith prove the steps; Isar provides the structure that a person reads.
9

What you learned

  • Isar writes proofs as structured text: each step states what is true, and Isabelle checks it. Such proofs can be read without Isabelle, and a failing step is marked where it fails.
  • A proof block proof m … qed starts with an initial method: proof (rule r), proof (a standard rule for the goal) or proof - (nothing). Inside it, assume takes a premise, fix an arbitrary value, have proves an intermediate fact and show solves a pending goal. next separates the proofs of different goals.
  • Facts flow from step to step: then passes this, from a passes named facts, with a passes both, using a comes after the claim, note collects facts, and moreover … ultimately collects several before using them. hence and thus are old forms of then have and then show. A method receives the passed facts and can also use its configured context rules and the subgoal’s premises.
  • obtain x where … uses an existential fact, as the rule (∃E) does.
  • Calculations chain steps with also and end them with finally; … stands for the previous right-hand side, and . directly uses the fact that the chain produced. For numeric chains mixing =, ≤ and <, the result has the strictest relation of the chain.
  • Case analysis uses proof (cases …) with case True/case False or the constructors of a datatype, consider for cases of your own, and proof with a disjunction passed to it.
  • Induction uses proof (induction x) with case Nil, case (Cons x xs), case 0, case (Suc n); inside a case, ?case is its claim, c.IH the induction hypothesis and c.prems the premises.
  • Natural-deduction proofs and apply scripts translate into Isar step by step, and Isabelle’s messages (Failed to finish proof, Failed to refine any pending goal, Undefined case, …) point to the step to repair.

Exercise 10.4★★Disjunction the Isar way

Module 7 proved A ∨ B ⟶ B ∨ A with erule disjE. Prove it in Isar, with a case split on the assumed disjunction as in Section 10.5.3. Use blast only for the steps inside the cases.

Show solution
Isabelle
lemma "A ∨ B ⟶ B ∨ A"
proof
  assume "A ∨ B"
  then show "B ∨ A"
  proof
    assume "A"
    then show "B ∨ A" by blast
  next
    assume "B"
    then show "B ∨ A" by blast
  qed
qed

The outer proof applies impI. The inner proof receives the disjunction through then, so it applies the elimination rule disjE and leaves two goals, one for each case. The steps inside the cases are (∨I₂) and (∨I₁); by (rule disjI2) and by (rule disjI1) work as well.

Exercise 10.5★★Swap the quantifiers

Prove (∃x. ∀y. R x y) ⟶ (∀y. ∃x. R x y) in Isar with obtain and fix. Read the formula in words first: if one x is related to every y, then every y has some x related to it.

Show solution
Isabelle
lemma "(∃x. ∀y. R x y) ⟶ (∀y. ∃x. R x y)"
proof
  assume "∃x. ∀y. R x y"
  then obtain a where a: "∀y. R a y" by blast
  show "∀y. ∃x. R x y"
  proof
    fix b
    from a have "R a b" by (rule spec)
    then show "∃x. R x b" by (rule exI)
  qed
qed

The proof uses (∃E), (∀I), (∀E) and (∃I) in this order. The same x, here a, works for every y. The converse does not hold: each y may need a different x.

Exercise 10.6★★A calculation

Prove (a + b) * (a - b) = a * a - b * b for integers a and b with a chain of equations, one law per step, as in Section 10.4.1.

Show solution
Isabelle
lemma
  fixes a b :: int
  shows "(a + b) * (a - b) = a * a - b * b"
proof -
  have "(a + b) * (a - b) = a * (a - b) + b * (a - b)" by (rule distrib_right)
  also have "… = (a * a - a * b) + (b * a - b * b)" by (simp add: right_diff_distrib)
  also have "… = a * a - b * b" by (simp add: algebra_simps)
  finally show ?thesis .
qed

right_diff_distrib is the law a(b − c) = ab − ac. The last step uses commutativity, b · a = a · b, so that the middle terms cancel.

Exercise 10.7★★The total of a reversed bill

Prove mysum (rev xs) = mysum xs by induction with named cases. Use the lemma mysum_append of Section 10.6.1 in the induction step, inside a calculation.

Show solution
Isabelle
lemma "mysum (rev xs) = mysum xs"
proof (induction xs)
  case Nil
  show ?case by simp
next
  case (Cons x xs)
  have "mysum (rev (x # xs)) = mysum (rev xs @ [x])" by simp
  also have "… = mysum (rev xs) + mysum [x]" by (rule mysum_append)
  also have "… = mysum xs + x" using Cons.IH by simp
  also have "… = mysum (x # xs)" by simp
  finally show ?case .
qed

The second step is an instance of mysum_append, with rev xs for xs and [x] for ys; rule finds the instance. Without the lemma, simp would get stuck at mysum (rev xs @ [x]).

Exercise 10.8★★★Mirror a tree

Module 5 defined binary trees and the function mirror, and proved on paper, in the example Mirroring twice, that mirroring a tree twice gives the tree back. Here are the definitions again:

Isabelle
datatype 'a tree2 = Tip | Node "'a tree2" 'a "'a tree2"

fun mirror :: "'a tree2 ⇒ 'a tree2" where
  "mirror Tip = Tip"
| "mirror (Node l x r) = Node (mirror r) x (mirror l)"

Prove mirror (mirror t) = t in Isar by induction on t, with named cases. Write the induction step as a calculation with the three steps of the paper proof. How many induction hypotheses does the induction step have, and how do you refer to each one?

Show solution
Isabelle
lemma "mirror (mirror t) = t"
proof (induction t)
  case Tip
  show ?case by simp
next
  case (Node l x r)
  have "mirror (mirror (Node l x r)) = mirror (Node (mirror r) x (mirror l))" by simp
  also have "… = Node (mirror (mirror l)) x (mirror (mirror r))" by simp
  also have "… = Node l x r" using Node.IH by simp
  finally show ?case .
qed

The cases are named after the constructors, Tip and Node. A node has two subtrees, so the induction step has two induction hypotheses, one for l and one for r. Node.IH is the list of both; Node.IH(1) is the one for l and Node.IH(2) the one for r. The first two steps use the second equation of mirror, the third uses both hypotheses.

Exercise 10.9★★★Filtering never lengthens a list

filter P xs keeps the elements of xs that satisfy P. Prove length (filter P xs) ≤ length xs by induction on xs. In the induction step, split on whether P x holds, with a nested proof (cases "P x"), and use a calculation in the case where it does not.

Show solution
Isabelle
lemma "length (filter P xs) ≤ length xs"
proof (induction xs)
  case Nil
  show ?case by simp
next
  case (Cons x xs)
  show ?case
  proof (cases "P x")
    case True
    then show ?thesis using Cons.IH by simp
  next
    case False
    then have "length (filter P (x # xs)) = length (filter P xs)" by simp
    also have "… ≤ length xs" by (rule Cons.IH)
    also have "… ≤ length (x # xs)" by simp
    finally show ?thesis .
  qed
qed

Inside the nested block, ?thesis is the claim of the show it proves, which is the claim of the case. In the case where P x holds, filter keeps x, and both sides grow by one; the hypothesis does the rest.

Check yourself

1
In then have "B" by blast, what does then do?
then is short for from this: the fact produced by the previous step is passed to the method that proves the next claim.
2
Which command solves a pending goal of the enclosing block?
have only adds a fact. show claims a statement and, once its proof is finished, uses it to solve a pending goal.
3
In also have "… = x + 1", what does … stand for?
… abbreviates the right-hand side of the previous fact, so the chain continues from where it stopped.
4
proof fails on the goal False, while proof - works. Why?
Without a method, proof applies a standard introduction rule chosen by the goal. proof - applies no rule, so the steps inside the block have to prove the goal.
5
In case (Cons x xs) of a proof by induction xs, what is Cons.IH?
The induction step assumes the claim for the shorter list. Cons.prems holds the premises, and ?case the claim to prove.
6
Isabelle reports “Failed to refine any pending goal” at a show. What is the most likely cause?
The claim itself was proved, but it does not fit any goal. Compare it with the goal in the state, and check next, ?case and the assumptions.
